## ADR-022 — Services are a declared layer of the plan: nine additive build layers over naked walls, a run that stands on the wall face, and the control center split into two sealed chambers (2026-09-19)

- status: accepted. It closes the deferral **brief §7.4** has carried since plan v2 — the mandatory split of the control center into two isolated compartments — and with it the `open` note R08 carries in `plan.ts`, which is deleted rather than reworded because the thing it records is paid off here. It records the owner's eight answers of 2026-09-19 (`.claude/claude.questions.md` Batch 4, reproduced verbatim in `TASKS.md` Part 5) and the room-by-room programme he dictated with them. It **builds on ADR-021 and reopens none of it**: a wall is still one thickness for its whole height.
- context: The floor says what is **built**. It says nothing about what **runs** through it, and the owner asked for both — the building behind the plaster, and a viewer that peels it: naked walls at the bottom, the finished look at the top, one service between each, in the order a building is actually built. Three things already in the repository made this a routing problem rather than a design problem, and none of them was re-derived. **The centre exists**: R08/CTR, `[1.60, 3.80, 7.20, 9.70]`, 2.20 × 2.50 = 5.50 m², holding one `servicesCabinet` at `[1.65, 3.05, 9.05, 9.65]` that Part 4 declared as **one box and not six** precisely so the deferred split would have a single object to divide. **The vertical zone exists**: the side-B strip is 0.80 m deep for a stated reason — it carries the water, gas and electricity risers — and both `voidWest` `[4.90, 11.65, 8.90, 9.70]` and `voidEast` `[15.35, 20.30, 8.90, 9.70]` already carry the note `drying line and service risers; plumber access`. **The way out exists**: `controlCenter ↔ ccBalcony`, a 0.70 m door at z 8.95, onto the west end of that same side-B row, which is why the horizontal spine is the owner's own sentence — everything crosses the balcony. What forced the decision **now** rather than at some later part is that the owner put the **central water heater** in R08. Heater, gas, electricity and water in one 5.50 m² room is the full combination, and "serious separation" was the instruction; a split that could be deferred while the room held a cupboard cannot be deferred once it holds a burner.
- decision: **Ten things, and the first is the one the other nine hang off.** (1) **Services are a declared layer of the plan, not a drawing.** `SERVICE_LAYERS`, `SERVICE_SPEC`, `SERVICE_CHAMBERS` and `SERVICE_RUNS` live in `plan.ts` beside `PORTS`, `WINDOWS` and `FIXTURES`, so `pnpm verify:plan` can check a pipe the same way it checks a door, and `domain/services.ts` gives a run its third dimension the way `domain/fixtures.ts` gives a fitting its elevation (ADR-015 is the precedent and the pattern copied). A run declares `layer`, `family`, its two ends, a centreline of `[x, z, y]` points on the centimetre grid written **in the order the service flows**, and an optional `why`; `y` is measured from the finished floor of the storey, so a negative `y` is inside the floor build-up — the 0.30 m between `HEIGHTS.wall` (2.70) and `HEIGHTS.floorToFloor` (3.00) — which is where waste actually runs, and a riser needs no vocabulary of its own because it is two points differing in `y`. An end is a space, a chamber, a **named fitting**, or a **cap**. The matricule tag is **`S`**: `W`, `P`, `G` and `X` are taken by walls, ports, glazing and fixtures and `R` by the rooms, and a run is numbered `F1-DRN-S4` — floor, a three-letter layer code (`DRN` · `WTR` · `GAS` · `ELE` · `ELV` · `CLM`), then the `S` number. Three letters because that is the width the plan already numbers rooms in, and `ELV` rather than a code derived mechanically from `lowVoltage` because extra-low voltage is what the trade calls it. The code table is **total over the layer keys**, and the three `undefined` entries are the point: a tenth layer cannot be added without somebody deciding whether pipes run in it. (2) **Nine layers, additive, nothing ticked is naked walls.** `drainage` · `water` · `gas` · `electricity` · `lowVoltage` · `climate` · `covers` · `furniture` · `finishing`, which is simultaneously the build order and the order the checkboxes appear in. Layers are things you **combine** — water over naked walls, then water and gas, then everything but the finish — so they are checkboxes and never a dropdown, and **all nine ticked reproduces v1.0.0 pixel for pixel**. The last three are in the list because the owner asked for **a box per build level**, not because they are services, and each carries `service: false` so that the distinction is data rather than a convention: `covers` is the boxing built over a run, `furniture` is the 44 fixtures, `finishing` is ADR-020's decorative scheme. (3) **A run is its own declared solid standing ON the wall face.** It is never a modification of a wall. ADR-021 priced per-height wall thickness and rejected it, and the food-pass tunnel had to be a fixture for exactly that reason; a buried chase, a duct in a bulkhead and a riser boxed into a corner are the same shape of problem and inherit the same answer. This is also what the owner asked for in as many words — "anything selected is top of the wall". **Do not reopen it.** (4) **The control-center split is paid off, as two sealed chambers inside one room.** R08's rects do not move, the floor stays **21 spaces**, and every total holds: `exact: floor 163.9200 · void 9.3600 · stairwell 8.0000 · walls 43.7200`, closing on 225.000 m² of plot. The single `servicesCabinet` becomes `wetGasChamber` `[1.65, 2.30, 9.05, 9.65]` — heater, gas cock, cold and hot manifolds — and `electricalChamber` `[2.40, 3.05, 9.05, 9.65]` — consumer unit, meters, low-voltage patch — each a **closed box 2.20 m tall**, each with a `holds` list that is the isolation rule itself, checkable rather than conventional: a gas run ending in the electrical compartment is the single failure this part exists to prevent. **Each vents to a different outside face** — wet and gas **west** into `balconyA`, electrical **east** into `ccBalcony` — on a 125 mm duct, because a sealed cupboard with a burner in it has to breathe and venting it the other way would have carried a gas atmosphere over the electrical compartment. Low voltage shares the electrical box rather than getting a third, because what it must be kept away from is a cable laid beside it for metres, not a patch panel in the same cupboard. (5) **`water` is the domestic supply and `climate` is the comfort circuit.** `water` is cold and hot to fittings; `climate` is the cooling runs and the wall heaters with their flow and return. **A radiator is not a tap.** This is recorded because it is nowhere in the brief: the heaters run off the same chamber the hot water does, which is the reason it is tempting to file them under `water`, and the owner's own checkbox reads "climate — heating / cooling". **The two halves of that circuit have two different sources, and the cooling one is not a chamber at all.** The wall heaters start in `wetGasChamber`, because they run off the same heater the hot water does. The cooling starts at `{ at: 'space', space: 'ccBalcony' }`: the plant is an **outdoor unit standing on the control-center balcony**. A condenser rejects heat and needs open air, so it cannot stand in the sealed electrical box — and putting it in the wet-and-gas box would have stood a refrigeration unit next to a burner. It is the one service on this floor whose source is a **place rather than a chamber**, which is why its `from` names a space rather than naming one of the two compartments, and it is a real constraint rather than a detail of the routing: a chamber is defined here as a sealed box, and the one piece of plant that must not be sealed in is therefore outside the register by construction. (6) **`isServicedSpace` is NOT taught about runs.** A pipe crossing a bedroom does not make that bedroom a serviced space, and teaching it otherwise would lay white-veined marble under a bed. `SERVICING_ROLES` answers a question about what stands **in** a room and keeps answering only that. ADR-021 fixed exactly this class of defect once, one space wide and invisible from any aggregate, so the guard here is not an aggregate either: `floorLayout.test.ts` pins the floor finish of **all 21 spaces** by name, asserts that the marble set is exactly `SERVICED_SPACE_IDS`, and fails if a twenty-second space ever slips through unpinned. (7) **Services are read by hue, not by texture** — ADR-020's scheme is deliberately **inverted** inside these layers. The scheme's whole argument is that unrelated things share a material; a services view exists to answer the opposite question, which is a plumber's first question and is gas-or-water. So the ten service material keys are flat colour with no `map` looked up for any of them, following real service-colour convention where one exists (drainage the dark unglamorous one, cold and hot the blue and red halves of one question, gas the near-universal yellow, cooling cyan) and choosing for **separability** where none does — the heating hue, because yellow, orange and red were already spoken for and a fourth warm hue would have been the conventional answer and an unreadable one. Power and lighting share a hue on purpose: they are one service, and the circuit family is carried as data. (8) **The ends of the stack are capped and the model says so.** The building is 1…10 identical storeys (ADR-014), so a riser declared once is automatically in the same place on every one of them — but the ends of the stack are not automatic. Floor 0 is undesigned and the roof is not modelled, so a run that simply stopped would be a pipe ending in mid-air. Each end is an `at: 'cap'` carrying its **reason in the data** — pending the floor 0 design at the bottom, pending the roof plant at the top, each saying when it is to be deleted — exactly the way both half-flights at the ends of the stair stack are blocked but still drawn. (9) **Real bores, sized as a plumber advises, with the narrowing stated.** 110 mm soil, 40 mm waste, 50 mm on a shower gully because a tray takes a flood rather than a bowlful, 75 mm stack vent, 125 mm chamber vent; 25 mm supply risers and 20 mm branches; falls of 1:80 on soil and 1:40 on waste and gullies, stated as minima because too shallow leaves solids standing and too steep lets the water outrun them. On top of that a **`narrowingAllowance`**, which is not geometry and would have been invented if it were not written down: a pipe does not keep its bore, scale closes it from the inside, and it closes **faster on hot** because that is where the scale comes out of solution — so a supply pipe is specified one allowance over the flow it must carry and **hot carries the larger of the two** (0.2 against cold's 0.1). It is the owner's own instruction. (10) **The level of detail is itself a decision.** Wet services end at a **real fitting** — every basin, bath, shower, cistern, machine and the cooker, at its real outlet, falling the whole way, because "build everything like reality, even the bathroom-out". Electricity, ethernet and climate end at a **room**, with `spareCapacityFraction` (0.4) of every conduit left free, because what the owner asked for is capacity arriving in every room so that smart-home kit and extra outlets are a later addition and not a re-route — not a cable drawn to a socket nobody has chosen. Two distances are checks rather than comments for the same reason a comment does not fail a build: `dataToPowerSeparation` at 0.20 m, which is the air a mains cable needs before it stops inducing noise into an unshielded pair laid beside it, and the 0.05 m minima between gas and power and between water and power.
- alternatives: **modelling a run as a chase in the wall**, which is per-height wall thickness under another name — ADR-021 priced that change across the plan schema, `deriveWalls`, the drawing generator and both wall derivations ADR-012 keeps apart, and rejected it for a 0.70 m stub; it is no cheaper for a pipe, and `PARAPET_WALLS` still only lowers a whole face; **one drainage stack instead of three**, which is a branch crawling the length of the building losing height the whole way — the wet rooms fall into three groups metres apart, so there is a west stack in `voidWest` and a laundry stack and a main stack in `voidEast`, each standing in a void and never in a room's clear floor nor in `balconySlabB`, which is the one floored part of that row and is walked on; **folding `drainage` into `water`**, which is one service to a viewer and two to a plumber — supply can be routed for convenience and a drain cannot, and merging them also makes `water` unreadable now that it carries cold and hot; **folding the wall heaters into `water`** because they leave the same chamber (see decision 5); **a dropdown instead of checkboxes**, which allows exactly one answer and would have to be widened into a multi-select the first time anyone used it — a checkbox list with a smaller hit target; **making the layers exclusive**, same objection from the other end; **a third chamber for low voltage** (decision 4); **venting both chambers to the same face**, which is the one arrangement the split exists to prevent; **splitting R08 into two rooms** rather than two chambers inside one room — it moves rects that every total, both dimension chains and the 21-space count depend on, to model something that is a cupboard and not a room; **teaching `isServicedSpace` about runs**, which is the defect ADR-021 fixed, restated; **special-casing the rooms a pipe crosses** in a hand-kept list, which is the same defect with a maintenance burden attached; **applying ADR-020's scheme inside the service layers** (decision 7); **schematic bores**, which is a drawing rather than a model and makes every clearance check meaningless; **raw nominal bores with no narrowing allowance**, which is the number a catalogue prints and not the number a plumber specifies; **ending the electrical and data runs at sockets**, which is inventing the owner's fittings for him and freezes an installation he explicitly asked to be able to extend; **leaving the risers to stop at the top and bottom slab with no cap**, which is a pipe ending in mid-air and reads as a modelling bug rather than as a pending design; **the layer state in the URL**, which is a new rule about the whole app — nothing else in this app is in the URL — and is revisitable if a naked-walls view ever has to be sent as a link; and **`U` instead of `S` for the matricule tag**, held in reserve in case `S` read too close to "stair" to the owner, and not needed: a room's type code is three letters, so nothing already on this floor can be confused with `F1-DRN-S4`.
- consequences: **The plan gained 116 declared runs** — 13 drainage, 18 water, 2 gas (the cooker and one chamber vent), 39 electricity, 16 low voltage, 28 climate (9 cooling, 19 heating) — which the domain module builds into **219 segments, 203 covers and 9 caps**, and every one of them is data the verifier can fail on rather than a line on a drawing. **`wetGasChamber.holds` therefore lists `climate` for its heating half only**, which is the one entry in the isolation register that does not mean the whole layer and has to be read with decision 5 beside it. **Findings came out of the routing itself, and they are the sort that get buried in a commit message.** _The kitchen had no sink._ It held two counters, a cooker and a fridge and **nothing to connect either water or waste to**, which nobody had noticed because a kitchen is obviously a kitchen; the floor-finish pin found the same hole from the other side, since the kitchen reads as serviced only on the strength of its cooker and its fridge. A sink was added and the west counter run shortened by 0.65 m to take it — a 600 mm sink unit with its worktop margins — so `FIXTURES` goes **42 → 44**: one cabinet became two chambers, plus the sink. _A sloped segment is legal and necessary._ The first draft of the domain rule was the obvious one, "a segment may change exactly one axis", and it would have **rejected the plan as it stands**: every drain in the building changes a horizontal axis and `y` together, because a drain falls as it runs — the WC branch travels 2.05 m north and drops 0.03 m doing it. What is not buildable on this grid is a **plan diagonal**, a segment changing x and z together, which has to be declared as two legs, and that is what `assertRunAxis` throws for. A falling leg is drawn as a level box containing the sloped pipe, spanning the fall plus the bore, which is the vocabulary every other module of this domain is drawn in. **Two more came out of building the runs rather than declaring them, and the first is the reason decision 5 has a paragraph about a condenser in it.** _Two cooling trunks started in the electrical chamber._ `electricalChamber.holds` is `['electricity', 'lowVoltage']`, so a `climate` run terminating in it is **precisely the isolation failure this whole split exists to prevent** — and it got there the dullest way possible, by the cooling trunk being copied from the electrical trunk beside it and its `from` never being changed. Nothing human caught it: it was found by the domain module reading the declaration arithmetically, and no test that existed at the time would have failed on it. That is the argument for `holds` being a checked register rather than a convention, made by the register catching its author. _The cooling boxing stood 15 mm above the ceiling._ The duct is 160 mm and its lane was at a centreline of 2.60 m, so the run plus its cover broke the 2.70 m ceiling by 0.015 m — invisible in a plan view, and a hole in the room from inside it. The lane dropped to **2.55**. Both defects are of the same species: a number that is correct for the service it was copied from and wrong for the one it was pasted into, which is what a verifier over declared data is for. **The cost of the sealed chambers is that the control center is now mostly plant**: two closed boxes 2.20 m tall stand in a 5.50 m² room whose north leaf is 1.20 m wide on the owner's own instruction, and a technician reaching them still crosses a bedroom — the accepted cost ADR-011 recorded, unchanged and now with more behind the door. **A buried run gets no cover**, asked per segment rather than per run, so a stack that starts under the floor and rises through an open void is boxed over only the part a person can see; this is exactly the rule a reviewer will tidy into consistency, and a test pins it. **What this entry decides is the model and the switcher; the render is still outstanding at the time of writing.** `plan.ts` carries the runs, `domain/services.ts` builds them, `application/layerStore.ts` holds a flag per layer starting at all-off and `ui/LayerSwitcher.tsx` puts the nine checkboxes in the HUD — but the layer-to-render wiring is being built alongside this ADR, so nothing here should be read as a claim about what is on screen today: the service buckets in `floorLayout.ts` exist and the runs are not yet in them, no bucket is yet gated on its layer flag, and the empty-layer state Part 5 requires is not written. When that lands, **the default view of the app becomes a different building** — naked walls, every box unticked — which means both screenshot baselines and the README images change, and a wrong baseline still passes every run after a human commits it (ADR-019). **The counts move again.** `pnpm verify:plan` gains checks and its PASS line, `TASKS.md`'s header, `docs/RUNBOOK.md`, `README.md` and `eslint.config.js` all state a check count (ADR-010 does not, despite being named as a carrier); a stale one is the true-looking string ADR-021's consequences section warns about, and the fixture count in the same sentences moves 42 → 44 with it. **And nine layers is nine chances to make the floor cost nine times as much to draw**: the merge is per material key, so a layer is a bucket that is either handed to the renderer or not, and the day that stops being true the switcher stops being a toggle and becomes a rebuild of the whole floor.

## ADR-021 — The guest suite gives up its shower, the guest room becomes a sitting room, the food pass is a tunnel built as a fixture, and a dry fixture is no longer a reason to lay marble (2026-09-19)

- status: accepted. It closes the `OPEN` item brief §7.3 has carried since plan v2 — by restoring §7.3's own table rather than by spending either of the two levers §7.3 named — and it **supersedes one clause of ADR-020**: the sentence "a room is serviced when it holds anything that is not furniture". The rest of ADR-020 stands.
- context: The owner walked the finished floor in 3D on 2026-09-19 and came back with three things, and they turn out to be one thing. **The guest suite was too tight**, which is exactly the question §7.3 left open ("if the guest suite proves too tight in the 3D walk-through, the levers are to drop its bath or take depth from the guest room") — the open part is 0.55 m deep against a 0.50 m walker, so it has 0.05 m of lateral room along its whole length, and `tour.spec.ts` had already had to walk the suite last because threading back _out_ of a 0.70 m cubicle across that band to the far end of the floor is not reliable. **The guest room had the wrong furniture in it**: he asked for the "yellow thing" to go — a 0.60 × 0.90 × 2.20 m oak wardrobe against the east wall, the tallest object in a room 1.55 m deep — and for somewhere for guests to actually sit. **The food pass read as a hole, not a hatch**: "not just a window, a tunnel, hosting two windows — one on the kitchen side, one on the guest side — and just the bottom, the top keep it a normal wall." Behind all three is the same fact, which is that §7.3's own table has always said `Guest Sanitair | Sink (open) + Bath — **NO shower**`, and the suite that was built has a shower in it. It was added later, when the concept was made literal and the bath and the shower became rooms with their own sliding doors; nobody recorded a decision to add it, and it is the single thing that made this corner of the floor the tightest thing on it.
- decision: **Three, and the first pays for the other two.** (1) **The guest shower is deleted.** `guestShowerCubicle` — R20/SHW, `[8.85, 9.85, 7.90, 8.60]`, 0.70 m² — is gone, which takes the floor from 22 spaces to **21** and renumbers the two family cubicles behind it: `mainBathCubicle` R21 → **R20** and `mainShowerCubicle` R22 → **R21**. This is treated as a _third_ lever, not as one of §7.3's two: the brief's levers both cost something the owner asked for, and this one costs something the brief never asked for in the first place. The metre it frees is spent east-to-west. The whole suite slides onto the shower's floor — `guestSanitair` is `[8.05, 9.85, 7.20, 7.75]` (1.54 → **0.99 m²**) and `guestBathCubicle` is `[8.05, 9.85, 7.90, 8.60]` (1.15 → **1.26 m²**, because the cubicle absorbs the 0.15 m partition that used to separate it from the shower and is 1.80 m wide where it was 1.65) — and the guest room's lower leg grows into the west end the suite vacates, `[4.10, 7.90, 7.05, 8.60]` where it was `[4.10, 6.90, …]`, taking R09 from 10.42 to **11.97 m²**. The basin changes ends with the room, from the west dead-end to the east one at `[9.50, 9.85, 7.25, 7.70]`, for the reason that put it in a dead-end at all: it has to stand where no leaf does. Both leaves are at the **west** end now and very nearly opposite one another — the room door in the north face at x 8.10–8.80, the bath door in the south face at x 8.20–8.80 (`spanMin: 8.2`) — which puts the whole suite's circulation in one 0.75 m of the run and leaves the east dead-end to the basin alone. (2) **The guest room is a SITTING room, not a guest bedroom.** The bed and the wardrobe are deleted and replaced by a `sofa` at `[4.20, 6.40, 7.75, 8.55]` and a freestanding `coffeeTable` at `[4.75, 5.85, 7.25, 7.65]`. The wardrobe went by name. The bed went by arithmetic: the leg is **1.55 m deep**, a bed is 0.90 and a sofa is 0.80, and 0.90 + 0.80 = 1.70 does not fit across 1.55 however the two are turned — widening the leg to 3.80 bought _length_, not depth, so it was a bed or a sofa and the owner chose the sofa. One number in that pair was not chosen for comfort: the table's north edge is at z 7.25 because the stairwell leaf swings 0.90 m into this room, to z 7.20, and drawn at 7.15 the table took 0.04 m² of that swing and check 9 caught it. (3) **The food pass is a 1.00 m tunnel, and the missing 0.70 m of it is a FIXTURE.** The `pass` window narrows from 0.65 to **0.55** (`spanMin` 6.35 → 6.40; sill 1.00 and head 1.80 unchanged), which buys 0.10 m of masonry jamb each side instead of the bare 0.05 m minimum, and a new fixture kind **`passCounter`** at `[9.00, 9.70, 6.30, 7.05]` with `along: 'x'` builds the other 0.70 m out from the guest-room face. The opening therefore has a mouth at each end and a ledge at 1.00 m to stand plates on, the masonry stays 0.30 m, and the tunnel stops at 1.90 m with plain wall above it. It is a fixture **because a wall in this model cannot be what the owner asked for**: a wall here is the gap between two room rects and its thickness is ONE number for its whole height, so "1.00 m thick up to the pass head, 0.30 m above it" cannot be declared — moving the guest room's east face to x 9.00 would build a 1.00 m wall all the way to the ceiling — and `PARAPET_WALLS` only lowers a whole wall face, which would leave an open slot over the pass rather than the normal wall he specifically asked for. The bore's axis is **stated** on the fixture rather than inferred from the rect: 0.70 × 0.75 turns on a 0.05 m margin, so deducing it from the longer side would silently rotate the tunnel 90° the next time the opening moved a centimetre (`heightAt` in `walls.ts` settled the same argument for wall heights, and `PORTS` and `WINDOWS` already carry a field of this name and type). (4) **A fifth fixture role, `joinery`, and `isServicedSpace` asks a positive question.** The pass counter surfaced a defect in how ADR-020's floor rule was written down. ADR-020 _decided_ that a room is carpeted unless something in it is plumbed, powered or a riser, and _implemented_ that as "anything that is not furniture" — which was a faithful paraphrase only while `fitting` meant both "plumbed in" and "built in". A pass counter is dry carpentry: nothing runs to it, no water, no drain, no power. Under the negation it would have laid a bathroom's white-veined marble across a guest sitting room. So `PlanFixtureRole` gains **`joinery`** — built in like a fitting, but dry — `counter` and `passCounter` are both classified that way, and `isServicedSpace` now asks a new exported frozen set, **`SERVICING_ROLES` = `fitting` | `appliance` | `services`**, instead of asking what a thing is not. The kitchen counter moved with the pass counter rather than being left behind: it is the same kind of object, and a role that held one of the two would be a role invented for one room. The owner was shown the choice and chose **carpet** under the sofa. **No room on the floor changes finish except the guest room** — the kitchen is still marble, serviced by its cooker and its fridge, and every bathroom, the laundry and the control center are unmoved.
- alternatives: **spending one of §7.3's two stated levers** — dropping the guest _bath_ is dropping the one fitting §7.3's table actually asks the guest suite to have, and taking depth from the guest room takes it either from the north strip, which is circulation and is the only way to the control center, the side-A balcony and the suite itself (ADR-011 accepted §6's access rule on the strength of that strip), or from the lower leg, which is where the sofa had to go; both levers are left **unspent and still available** if the suite ever needs more; **accepting the tightness and recording it**, which is what the previous state did and what the earlier `tour.spec.ts` comment claimed the owner had chosen — the 3D walk-through was the test §7.3 set, and it came back no; **keeping the bed and adding the sofa beside it** (1.70 m of furniture across a 1.55 m leg); **keeping the wardrobe and dropping something else** (it is 2.20 m tall in a 1.55 m deep room, and it is the object the owner named); **leaving the two family cubicles at R21/R22 with R20 vacant** — a matricule is an index into the plan's own order and not a name, and a hole in it is a trap for anything that reads the sequence, but the renumber is not free and `tour.spec.ts` shows why: its guest-suite list named `F1-R20`, which now denotes the **family** bath at the far east end of the floor, so left alone it would have quietly reordered the wrong room while still reading as three correct matricules; **building the tunnel as masonry** by moving the guest room's east face west to x 9.00 (a 1.00 m wall to the ceiling — the exact thing the owner ruled out); **`PARAPET_WALLS`** (lowers a whole face, leaving an open slot above the pass); **giving the plan a per-height wall thickness** so a wall could be 1.00 m below the head and 0.30 above (it is a change to the plan schema, to `deriveWalls`, to the drawing generator and to both wall derivations ADR-012 keeps apart, for one 0.70 m stub — the fixture layer already models "a box installed in a room", which is what this is); **inferring the bore axis from the fixture's longer side** (see above: 0.05 m decides it); **leaving `isServicedSpace` as a negation and special-casing the guest room** in the pinned list (that is a list maintained by hand against a rule that no longer says what it means — the defect would come back with the next dry fixture); **classifying only `passCounter` as `joinery` and leaving the kitchen counter a `fitting`** (the kitchen stays marble either way, on its cooker and its fridge, so the only thing that choice buys is a role that exists for exactly one object); and **marble in the guest room** — it is what the code would have done on its own, and the owner was asked rather than told.
- consequences: The floor has **21 spaces**, and every `F1-R20` / `F1-R21` / `F1-R22` string in the repository now means a different room than it did — this is the change most likely to pass review while being wrong, because the strings stay well-formed. `pnpm verify:plan` prints **92 walls** (was 96), **27 openings** (was 29), **19 ports** (was 20), **8 windows** (was 9) and 42 fixtures (unchanged), and its totals move to `exact: floor 163.9200 · void 9.3600 · stairwell 8.0000 · walls 43.7200` against the old `floor 163.5150 … walls 44.1250`; 225.000 m² still closes on the plot. ## ADR-020 — The scheme is the palette: one decorative scheme over the whole floor, and the per-space override deleted (2026-09-19)

- status: accepted, and it replaces the single-room scoping this same part introduced two days' work earlier. `SUPERSEDED IN PART 2026-09-19 — by ADR-021`: **one clause of the decision below no longer describes the code.** "That question is asked of the plan's own `FIXTURE_ROLES` (`isServicedSpace`), not of a list of room ids: a room is serviced when it holds **anything that is not furniture**" — the negation was a faithful paraphrase of "plumbed, powered or a riser" only while `fitting` meant both "plumbed in" and "built in". It stopped being one when the guest room's pass counter arrived: dry carpentry that the negation would have floored in marble. `isServicedSpace` now asks the positive question through `SERVICING_ROLES` (`fitting` | `appliance` | `services`), and the dry things are `joinery`. **The decision this ADR made is unchanged** — plumbed, powered or a riser means marble, everything else carpet, and no room's finish moved — and everything else in this entry stands.
- context: The owner gave a written brief for a scheme — flat matte white walls, natural unvarnished light oak millwork, bouclé upholstery and flat-weave carpet against white-veined marble, spidery matte-black fixtures, large-scale abstract artwork in muted pastel — and asked to see it in the living room first, as a test. It was built that way: four extra palette keys (`parisCarpet`, `parisOak`, `parisBoucle`, `parisMarble`) and a per-space override in `floorLayout.ts` that swapped a box's family for a scheme family when the box stood in `livingRoom`. He looked at it and asked for it over the whole floor. That makes the override machinery dead weight: an override that every space takes is not an override, it is the palette, and keeping both sets would leave four base colours — a cool dark furniture timber, a dark worktop stone, a muted textile grey — defined, tested, and painted onto nothing.
- decision: **Restyle the base families and delete the scheme families.** `joinery` is unvarnished light oak, `worktop` is white-veined marble, `softFurnishing` is bouclé, `wall` is flat matte white, `railing` is the brief's spidery matte black, and `stairs` is the same oak as the millwork one step more sealed, because a tread is walked on and a wardrobe door is not. `FloorMaterialKey` loses four members and the floor draws **fifteen** meshes where it drew nineteen: decorating twenty-two rooms costs four meshes _fewer_ than decorating one did, which is the clearest measure of how much of that machinery existed only to keep two palettes apart. Two consequences of the brief needed deciding rather than deriving, and both are recorded here because neither is visible from the brief's own words. **The floors.** Flat-weave carpet cannot go everywhere — not in a bathroom, not in a kitchen — so a room is carpeted unless something in it is plumbed, powered or a riser, in which case it is marble. That question is asked of the plan's own `FIXTURE_ROLES` (`isServicedSpace`), not of a list of room ids: a room is serviced when it holds anything that is not furniture. It is deliberately **broader than the wet-room rule it replaces**, and the kitchen is why — it has no basin on this floor, only a counter, a cooker and a fridge, so the wet rule would have carpeted it. The control center, one services cabinet in front of the gas, water and electricity risers, is the same case. Circulation is decided above that question and is boarded in oak, which keeps a corridor reading as circulation underfoot the way the two screeds used to, in the scheme's own materials. **The floor weights.** A floor is the same material as the millwork above it and never the same value: a stair tread cut from the landing it lands on has no edge, and a white basin on a white marble floor has no outline, which is a defect the single-room test could not surface because the living room has neither. So there is a board oak and a millwork oak, a floor marble and a worktop marble. Real floors are darker than the furniture standing on them anyway, so the legible answer and the physical one agree.
- alternatives: widening the override to every space (leaves the base colours dead and the mechanism pointless, and keeps two names for one oak); keeping both palettes and choosing per room (nobody asked for a second scheme, and a mechanism with one configuration is a guess about the future); carpeting every room including the wet ones (wrong, and visibly so); keeping the wet-room rule for the hard floors (it carpets the kitchen, which has no basin — the rule was answering a narrower question than a floor finish asks); one oak and one marble at a single value each (a tread would vanish into its landing and a basin into its floor); sharing one texture object between a wardrobe door and a corridor floor (the merged geometry gives every face the same 0…1 UV square whatever its real width, so one repeat cannot serve a 0.60 m door and a 10 m floor — the corridor's grain came out five metres from line to line); and leaving the railings brushed steel (the brief says matte black, and value separates more reliably than metalness does under a software rasteriser with no environment map).
- consequences: Both screenshot baselines and the three README images are regenerated, and every interior frame in the repository changes — this is a whole-building change to how the building looks, so that is the expected size of it. The palette test that required **every family to have a colour of its own** had to go: a scheme is precisely an agreement that unrelated things share a material, and two families now share the oak on purpose. It is replaced by two narrower tests that are actually true — the pairs that are seen touching may not share a colour, and the millwork and the treads share one by design — and `FloorModel.test.tsx` now identifies a mesh's bucket by colour, roughness and metalness together, since colour alone no longer names one. The old warm-beige palette is gone from the building, including from rooms nobody asked to decorate; if the owner wants a room back in the building's plain finish there is no longer a mechanism for it, and reintroducing one is a deliberate piece of work rather than a flag. `isWetSpace` stays in the domain and stays tested: a wet room is still a true and separate fact about a room, it is simply not the question a floor finish asks.

## ADR-019 — The screenshot baselines compare the whole frame, against a pixel count (2026-09-19)

- status: accepted, and it supersedes the masking rule recorded in ADR-008 and the diff ratio ADR-013 relied on
- context: Two committed frames are supposed to be the gate on "did the geometry move", and neither was answering that question. **The interior baseline masked 70 % of its own frame** — measured, 645 120 magenta pixels of 1280 × 720, rows 0 to 503 — because the mask was one box found as the overlay `<div>` that wraps every HUD panel, and that box had grown every time the HUD gained a panel. Part 3.5's olive slab soffit sat in the masked part: a Chrome sweep caught it, the baseline could not, and nothing about the baseline said it had stopped looking. **The tolerance was a 1 % diff-pixel ratio**, which reads like a small number and means 9 216 pixels of a full frame, and against the 30 % of the interior frame actually compared it was effectively 3.3 %. The proof that this is not theoretical is in the history: `exterior-default-chromium-linux.png` was written at `1351df3` and went **untouched through `b333280`**, the commit that rebuilt the entire floor on a new source of truth, moved the link corridor, deepened the stair bay and turned side B into a wall. The baseline passed a redrawn building.
- decision: **Hide the HUD for the duration of a capture instead of painting over it**, and compare the whole canvas with no mask at all. `src/app/App.tsx` carries `data-hud-overlay` on its one overlay div, documented there as a test contract; `tests/e2e/hideHud.css` keys on that same attribute and Playwright's `style` option applies it for the shot. The locator that counts the HUD and the stylesheet that hides it are now **the same string**, which is the structural answer to how the mask grew unnoticed — the two cannot drift apart. It also answers the objection the old docblock raised against masking each panel separately (a panel resizes with its text, so a mask of another size changes the frame by itself): nothing is painted over the frame at all. **The hide is `opacity: 0`, not `visibility: hidden`**, and the reason is recorded because it is not obvious and it cost a debugging session: `visibility` removes an element from the focus model, so the browser blurs whatever held focus inside it, and a capture taken mid-test silently moved focus off the view toggle and failed an assertion three lines later, deterministically, on a quiet machine. A screenshot helper may not change the state of the page it photographs. **The tolerance becomes an absolute `maxDiffPixels` of 200**, replacing the ratio: a ratio rescales with the viewport, so one number means different things at different sizes, and it reads as a percentage when what matters is how much of a building can move. The number is measured rather than chosen — both baselines were regenerated and then compared **20 times with the threshold set to zero, and all 20 passed**, so the software rasteriser is deterministic here and the noise floor is genuinely nothing. 200 is therefore a stated cushion for a future Mesa or driver change, about 0.02 % of the frame against the 9 216 the ratio allowed, and shipping the measured 0 would have been the more precise lie. A second guard, `expectHudWithinBudget`, asserts the overlay's own box stays within a declared share of the canvas, so HUD growth fails loudly instead of quietly eating the picture.
- alternatives: masking each panel individually rather than the whole overlay (the panels resize with their text, so the mask changes the frame by itself — the objection the original docblock raised, and it is correct); keeping the whole-box mask and merely asserting its size (it would catch future growth while leaving 70 % of today's frame uncompared); `display: none` for the hide (everything below the panels reflows, so the frame differs by more than the pixels the HUD painted); `visibility: hidden` (blurs the focused element — see above); tightening the ratio instead of replacing it (still a percentage, still rescaling with the viewport); splitting the canvas into regions and comparing each (a hand-maintained map of where the building is in frame, which breaks the moment the framing changes); asserting on geometry-derived pixels rather than the picture (a second implementation of the renderer living inside the test suite); and shipping the measured zero (any driver update becomes a red suite with no diagnosis, and a threshold nobody can interpret gets raised in a hurry by whoever is unlucky enough to hit it).
- consequences: Both baselines were regenerated, and they change for two reasons at once — the floor gained forty fixtures, and the frames no longer contain pink boxes. They were reviewed as images before being committed, which is the only gate that exists on a **wrong** baseline: `updateSnapshots: 'none'` stops CI writing one, but a human committing one still passes, and the RUNBOOK now says so rather than implying the automation covers it. The interior comparison goes from 30 % of the frame to 100 %, so it will now also fail on HUD-adjacent rendering that it previously could not see — which is the point, and which means a deliberate HUD change is a deliberate re-baseline. A loaded noise sample was attempted and abandoned: driving the machine to a load average near 30 starves the heavy interactive tests for reasons that have nothing to do with rendering, so it measures the machine rather than the picture, and re-measuring is a matter of re-running the idle protocol written into `playwright.config.ts`.

## ADR-018 — Self-hosted deploy: a release is a directory, going live is a rename, and the workflow is inert until a host exists (2026-09-19)

- status: accepted, and it closes the open item ADR-006 left
- context: ADR-006 recorded "Hosting: self-hosted; the concrete target and deploy workflow are decided in Part 4", and this is Part 4. Two facts shape the answer and neither is about hosting fashion. The build is **static**: Vite emits an `index.html` and a directory of content-hashed assets, with no server-side anything, so the deployable unit is a directory of files and the hard problems are cache control and how you put the new directory in front of people without a gap. And **GitHub Actions cannot bill on this repository**, which is why the owner's standing rule is already that a green local gate authorises a merge and `ci` is advisory. A deploy design whose only path runs on Actions would be a design that cannot be used the day it is written.
- decision: The target is **static files behind a web server on the owner's own host**, and the release unit is a **directory named after the commit**: `releases/<sha>/`, with the web root being a `current` symlink that points at one of them. Going live is therefore a single rename — the upload lands beside the live release and touches nothing the browser can see, then `ln -sfn` under a temporary name followed by `mv -Tf` swaps the symlink in one syscall. Plain `ln -sfn` over an existing symlink is **not** used, and the reason is recorded rather than left as folklore: it unlinks before it relinks, which leaves a window with no web root at all. Rolling back is the same rename pointed at the previous directory, with no rebuild, no upload and the bad release still on disk to be looked at; the pruner keeps the five newest. Cache control follows the hashed-asset contract the build already produces: `assets/*` is served `public, max-age=31536000, immutable` because every asset name carries its own hash, and `index.html` is served `no-store` because it is the one file whose name never changes and whose content always does, with `try_files $uri /index.html` so a deep link resolves. The transport is `rsync` over SSH with a **deploy-only key restricted by `command=` in the host's `authorized_keys`**, and the host is verified against a pinned `DEPLOY_KNOWN_HOSTS` rather than trusted on first use; no step ever echoes a secret. `.github/workflows/deploy.yml` runs exactly this sequence on a `v*` tag, and **every deploy step is guarded on `vars.DEPLOY_HOST` being set**, so a tag push on a repository with no host configured still builds, checks the bundle budget and uploads `dist/` as an artifact, and reports green rather than failing on a missing secret. The authoritative path meanwhile is **the same commands run by hand**, written out in `docs/RUNBOOK.md`'s new "Deploy" section — not a summary of the workflow but the commands themselves, so that restoring billing changes who runs them and nothing else.
- alternatives: building on the web host from a pulled tag (it puts a Node toolchain and a 2 700-test build on a public server, and makes the server's state a function of its own history); publishing a container image and having the host pull it (a second artifact store and a registry to keep credentials for, to ship a directory of static files); uploading a tarball as a release asset for the host to fetch (it needs Actions to publish the asset, so it inherits exactly the billing problem it was meant to route around); `rsync --delete` straight over the live directory (the site is broken for the length of the transfer, and a failed transfer leaves it broken); `--mkpath` to create the release directory (it needs rsync ≥ 3.2.3 **on the host**, which is not ours to guarantee, so the directory is made through `--rsync-path` instead); a managed static host (it is not self-hosted, which ADR-006 settled); and writing the workflow to fail loudly when no host is configured (a red run that means "not configured yet" teaches people to ignore red runs).
- consequences: The deploy exists as a reviewed, runnable procedure before a host does, which is the point — the owner can read it, disagree with it, and supply a hostname later without anything being rewritten. Four parameters are deliberately left open and named as such: the host, the SSH user, the document root and the TLS arrangement. Until they are filled, a `v*` tag produces a build, a budget check and an artifact, and nothing else; **`v1.0.0` is therefore a source release**, and the changelog says so rather than implying a live site. The symlink swap requires GNU coreutils on the host for `mv -T`, which is stated in the runbook beside the command. Keeping five releases means five copies of a ~1 MB directory, which is not worth managing more cleverly. And because the workflow and the runbook hold the same commands in two places, they can drift: the mitigation is that the runbook is the one a human reads and the workflow was written from it, so the workflow is what gets corrected if they ever disagree.

## ADR-017 — JSX accessibility linting is deferred: the plugin does not support ESLint 10, and four axe audits cover the rendered tree instead (2026-09-19)

- status: accepted
- context: Part 4's roadmap carries the line "Add eslint-plugin-jsx-a11y if it supports ESLint 10, otherwise record the deferral", which has been open since the bootstrap: ADR-001 already noted the plugin was skipped because there was no ESLint 10 release of it. The arithmetic has not moved. The latest published `eslint-plugin-jsx-a11y` is **6.10.2**, and it declares `peerDependencies.eslint: "^3 || ^4 || ^5 || ^6 || ^7 || ^8 || ^9"`; this repository runs `eslint ^10.10.0` with `@eslint/js ^10.0.1`. There is no version of the plugin that admits the linter we use.
- decision: Ship without it, and record the deferral here rather than let it live as a checkbox. The unblock condition is stated precisely so nobody has to re-derive it: **a release of `eslint-plugin-jsx-a11y` whose peer range admits ESLint 10**, at which point it is added to the `src/**/*.{ts,tsx}` block of `eslint.config.js` beside the React hooks config. The compensating control is named too, because a deferral without one is just a gap: `tests/e2e/accessibility.spec.ts` runs **four axe audits with no rule disabled** — the exterior view, the interior view, the interior with the room list open, and the whole HUD at a 400 px phone width — and it audits the _rendered_ accessibility tree, which is strictly more than a static JSX rule can see. A rule can tell you a `<div>` has an `onClick` and no role; it cannot tell you that a disclosure's `aria-controls` points at an element that is not in the document, or that a control ended up off-screen, both of which this project has actually shipped and caught elsewhere.
- alternatives: pinning ESLint back to 9 so the plugin installs (it would hold the whole lint toolchain at a major version behind to gain one plugin, and `@eslint/js` is already on 10); forcing the peer range with an override (a plugin run against an ESLint API it does not support produces a lint that lies, which is worse than no lint); writing a handful of the plugin's rules by hand (a worse subset, maintained here, competing with the real thing when it lands); adding a `.github/dependabot.yml` ignore entry so the gap is visible there (every entry in that file is an `ignore` for a package that **is** in `package.json`; there is nothing to ignore for one that was never installed, and a phantom entry rots unread); and leaving it as an unticked box in `TASKS.md` (a box says what was not done, an ADR says why, and this one has now been carried across four parts).
- consequences: Static JSX accessibility defects are not caught at lint time, and the project relies on the axe suite and on review for them. That is a real gap and the honest place to look for it is the components that build ARIA relationships by hand — the room menu's disclosure, the floor stepper's live reading, and the new room info panel. The RUNBOOK's "Lint / Format" section names this ADR and the one command that re-checks the premise, `pnpm view eslint-plugin-jsx-a11y peerDependencies`, and `eslint.config.js` carries a comment pointing here, so the next person who reaches for the plugin reads why it is absent before they try to add it.

## ADR-016 — Shipping the app: the scene loads on demand, the bundle has a budget, and a failure says so (2026-09-19)

- status: accepted, and it closes the deferral recorded in ADR-001
- context: ADR-001 accepted that "three.js makes the main chunk large; code splitting is deferred until real building content exists". It exists now, and the measurement was worse than the phrasing suggested: `pnpm build` emitted **one** JavaScript chunk of 1 417 603 bytes, 407.4 kB gzipped, 2.8× Vite's own 500 kB warning line, which every visitor downloaded in full before the page could draw its own toolbar. Separately, the app had **no error boundary, no WebGL capability check and no logging seam of any kind** — a grep for `Suspense`, `ErrorBoundary`, `componentDidCatch` or `lazy(` over `src` returned nothing. A browser without WebGL, a scene that threw while rendering and a lost GPU context all produced the same result: a blank canvas and silence.
- decision: **The 3D scene loads on demand.** `BuildingScene` — and through it three.js, drei and the merge pipeline — sits behind `React.lazy` and `Suspense`; every HUD component is three-free, so the shell stays in the entry chunk. The debug panel is lazied, and it stays **mounted** with a `visible` prop rather than being mounted only under `appConfig.showDebugPanel`. Gating the mount was tried, and it is the plan this ADR was first drafted against: it is wrong, because an unmounted `<Leva />` root makes leva inject an uncollapsed panel of its own over the HUD — which took the 400 px accessibility assertion red for a 30 s timeout. Lazying it buys nothing either way, and this ADR does not pretend otherwise: leva is fetched with the scene regardless, because `SceneLighting` imports `useControls` (see the debt below). The WebGL check runs **before** the lazy subtree, so a browser that cannot render never downloads what it cannot use. Initial JavaScript goes **407.4 kB → 91.8 kB gzip**. The chunks are **named** — react, three, r3f, leva — rather than left as nameless hashed files, because the budget below asserts against names and a glob whose meaning changes silently is not a gate. Two settings in that grouping were measured rather than assumed, and both are recorded because they look arbitrary and are not: without explicit priorities the `react` group came out **empty** while `r3f` swallowed 908 kB of three and react-dom; and capturing fiber's dependencies recursively pulled `zustand` — which is also the shell's own store — into the scene chunk, which the entry then statically imported, dragging 47.5 kB of drei back into the initial set. **The bundle has a budget**, in `tooling/bundleBudget.ts`, each number carrying the measurement it was set from and a stated headroom; `scripts/check-bundle-size.mjs` walks `index.html` → the entry → its **static** imports to compute what a visitor actually waits for, rather than looking for the biggest file, and it fails when no scene chunk exists at all, which is what catches the lazy boundary being quietly undone. It runs in CI immediately after the build, while `dist/` is still there. **Failures are now visible and said once.** `SceneErrorBoundary` catches a render-phase throw in the scene tree and a failed chunk; `detectWebGLSupport` decides before mounting; `SceneUnavailable` is a plain-DOM panel that carries no second `role="status"`. All four are mounted in `App.tsx` — stated here because for one commit they were not: they were built, tested and imported nowhere, and the review gate is what found it. The four together are why the initial chunk is 91.8 kB rather than 87.5: they have to be in it, since what they exist to render is the case where the scene chunk never arrives. All of it reports through **one module**, `app/observability/reportError.ts`, which is the only file allowed to touch the console — waived in `eslint.config.js` for that path alone rather than by an inline comment, because a comment is invisible from the config and travels with the line the first time someone copies it into a component. What is logged is deliberately thin: an event from a closed set, a message, a stack, a bag of primitives. No pose, no route, nothing about the viewer.
- alternatives: leaving the single chunk and raising `chunkSizeWarningLimit` to silence the warning (it throws away a free tripwire to avoid hearing it); splitting by route (there are no routes); `manualChunks` (deprecated in the rolldown-based Vite 8 here, its object form unsupported and its function form reaching a single group whose settings are unreachable); letting Rollup name the scene chunk itself (the budget would assert against a moving target); budgeting "the largest file" instead of the initial set (it measures the wrong thing — the largest file is `three`, which a visitor does not wait for); `rollup-plugin-visualizer` as the check (a devDependency and an HTML page a human has to read is not a gate); an inline `eslint-disable` for the console seam (invisible from the config, and copy-pasted); and claiming the error boundary catches everything (it cannot catch a throw from React Three Fiber's frame loop, which runs under `requestAnimationFrame` outside React's render, nor a lost context, which is an event and not a throw — so `BuildingScene` listens for that event itself and the boundary's docblock states the limit rather than overselling it).
- consequences: The canvas now appears after a chunk fetch, so first paint is the HUD rather than the scene; that is the right order — the toolbar is usable while three.js arrives — but it is a visible change in how the page loads. A dependency bump can turn the budget red, which is intended: a deliberate raise is a reviewed edit to `tooling/bundleBudget.ts` in the same pull request, and the failure prints the delta and names the file. **Leva still ships to a production page**, and this ADR does not fix it: `ui/SceneLighting.tsx` imports `useControls`, so the leva chunk is fetched with the scene however the debug flag is set. Removing that import is the only way to get it out, it touches the lighting ADR-009 made deterministic, and it is left as a recorded debt rather than done quietly alongside a release. The CI job's timeout went 25 → 40 minutes, because this part also adds a tour of every room to the end-to-end suite and the old ceiling would have gone red for arithmetic rather than for code.

## ADR-015 — Furnishing the floor: the footprint is plan data, the elevation is a domain module, and a fitting is a handful of boxes (2026-09-19)

- status: accepted
- context: Brief §7 lists the owner's equipment room by room — a washing machine and a hand-wash sink his mother prefers to the machine, two clothes armoires kept apart, cleaning storage, a kitchen that runs out onto a barbecue, a control center where every utility enters the building — and after four parts the plan held seven fixtures: six sanitary fittings and a television. A floor you can walk through with nothing in it cannot be judged against what has to fit in it, which is the whole question the owner asked this model to answer. Two things made this more than a data entry task. The first is that a fixture's rectangle is geometry, and ADR-010 allows exactly one copy of the geometry: put furniture anywhere but `plan.ts` and there are two places a rectangle lives, with nothing to keep them honest. The second is that the checks which make the plan trustworthy — check 9's containment, grid, overlap and door-swing tests — were written for seven objects and say nothing about whether a room is still usable once forty are in it. A wardrobe that blocks no door swing can still stand where you have to stand to open it.
- decision: **Footprints go into `plan.ts`; the third dimension becomes `domain/fixtures.ts`.** `PlanFixtureKind` grows from four kinds to twenty and `FIXTURES` from seven entries to about forty, so every new item is gated by `pnpm verify:plan` before any code sees it and appears on the generated drawing with its own matricule, which is how the owner corrects it (ADR-010's loop). `partition` is deliberately **not** declared: every division this layout needs is a real wall, cubicles included, and a kind with no member is a kind that invites one. The schema is what turns a rectangle into something checkable. A fixture now carries **`mount`** — `standing` backs onto a wall, `mounted` is flush with one, `freestanding` touches none — and the verifier tests the claim rather than trusting it, printing every freestanding item out loud the way it already prints exempt doors. It carries **`note`**, so the laundry's two armoires are not two indistinguishable rows in a register the owner reads. And it carries **`approach`**, an override of the 0.60 m of clear floor a fitting is entitled to in front of its working face, declared **only** where a room genuinely cannot give it — every reduction is one grep, and check 9 prints each one with the room that forced it, so a squeeze can never pass silently. `FIXTURE_ROLES` is a total record over the kind union, so a new kind cannot be added without being classified; that replaces `render-table.mjs`'s list of screen kinds, which silently defaulted anything it did not recognise. `compareFixturePosition` moves into the plan because a fixture's X-number is part of its matricule: the reading order is part of the identity scheme, not a presentation choice, and it existed twice in the drawing scripts before this. **Fixtures are drawn as procedural low-poly primitives** — one to three boxes each, sized by named constants in `domain/fixtures.ts` with the reason beside each, never external 3D assets: the building is derived from numbers the owner can argue with, and a downloaded mesh would be the one thing on the floor that is not. The three heights that already existed move across **unchanged**, because two committed screenshot baselines are pinned to them. Fixtures join `BuiltFloor` rather than arriving as a second layout folded in at the UI, which is what `floorLayout.ts` had been apologising for in a docblock since Part 2; `BuiltFloor` already carried the television panel, which is precisely a fitting. **The swing rectangle is extracted, not duplicated**: `domain/swingClearance.ts` now owns it and both `verify.mjs` and `ports/queries.ts` call it, so the one geometry that decides where a leaf may open has one implementation. And **fixtures are not collision blockers in this part**: "Go to room" routes to room centres and the route follower already steers through the tightest doorway on the floor with 0.05 m of slack, so a bed at a room centre would wedge it. That is a deferral with a reason, recorded here and in the backlog, not an oversight.
- alternatives: a separate furniture catalogue in `domain/fixtures.ts` with its own list of rectangles (two copies of the geometry, gated by a Vitest file that shares its own assumptions rather than by the verifier, and invisible on the drawing the owner marks up — the exact shape of the defect ADR-012 exists to record); leaving the sanitary heights in `ui/floorLayout.ts` and adding furniture heights beside them (the UI would keep deciding how tall a bath is, and the module's own docblock had already called that a Part 4 debt); putting fitting heights in `heights.ts` (its doc forbids it, and rightly: `floorToFloor` is a property of the building, a bath's rim is a property of a bath); a second TypeScript derivation of the door-swing rectangle to act as a check on the verifier's (ADR-012 accepted exactly that for the walls and the cost has been a standing footnote ever since — where a single derivation is available it is the better answer, and what remains genuinely split, which face a leaf hangs in, is pinned face by face from both sides instead); external 3D models for the furniture (they would look better and would sever the floor from the numbers that generate it); making fixtures solid this part (it needs the room targets re-derived, since several room centres now sit inside furniture, and it would break the walks the explore and floors suites already pin); declaring `approach` globally per kind rather than per fixture (it would bury the tight rooms in an average instead of naming them).
- consequences: The plan file is now long enough that a room's furniture is read through its comment block rather than by scanning the array, and that is the trade accepted for keeping one copy of the geometry. Four rooms could not simply be furnished and are recorded rather than quietly squeezed. **The guest sanitair's basin did not work as drawn** — 0.45 m deep in a 0.55 m room leaves 0.10 m to stand in — so it is rotated to a corner basin approached along the room's length; the owner's two stated levers, dropping its bath or taking depth from the guest room, both stay unspent, and the room keeps its `open` note so the 3D walk-through can judge it again. **The floor has no WC anywhere in the brief**, which furnishing it is what surfaced; the owner's answer is a pan in the main sanitair, and the guest suite having none is recorded on the room itself so `pnpm verify:plan` prints it and the room info panel shows it. **The barbecue** stands on a 0.80 m strip, so it is a 0.40 m built-in worked from the side rather than a grill you stand in front of. **The guest room takes a single bed**, laid along its length, and its north strip stays empty because it is the only route to the control center, the balcony and the kitchen pass. Two smaller things follow. The laundry's hand-wash sink makes it a **wet room** by the same rule that already decides the bathrooms, so its slab changes material — correct for a tiled laundry, and visible in the regenerated baselines. And the corridor is **deliberately left empty** but for the television: the appliance route needs its full 1.50 m, and check 9 would not catch a corridor narrowed by furniture, because blocking a corridor blocks no door swing. Finally, the drawing now carries about forty labels where it carried seven, and `render-plan.mjs` drops a label it cannot place; it used to do so silently, and the build script now counts and names every drop, because a drawing that is quietly incomplete is worse than one that says what it could not show.

## ADR-014 — Stacking the typical floor 1…10: the storey lives in the pose, a flight is a height function, and one geometry is drawn once per storey (2026-09-18)

- status: accepted, and it supersedes one clause of ADR-006
- context: ADR-006 recorded that the plan is the unified typical floor (floors 1…N) of an apartment building and then, in the same breath, that "the app shows floor 1 only, placed at level 0". The first half is the building; the second half was a scope line, and this part is where it expires. What forced decisions rather than a parameter is that nothing in the app carried a height. `EyePose` was `{x, z, yaw, pitch}` — there was no elevation in the movement model at all, and eye level was a constant re-applied at four render sites — so climbing was not a value to change but a dimension to add, and every consumer of the pose is a consumer of it. `SpaceId` stops naming a room the moment two storeys exist: `kitchen` is twenty rooms once the stack is ten deep, and a readout, a menu, a minimap and a walk failure message all name rooms. The exterior framing fitted a box exactly one storey tall. Against that, three things were already ready and are the reason this was cheap rather than a rebuild: `PlanBox` is level-agnostic, every derivation takes its `heights` injected rather than transcribed, and ADR-010 had already modelled the stair as running THROUGH the storey — flight A rising from this landing to the half-landing above, flight B arriving from the half-landing below, with `StairPiece.surfaces` built for a footprint that is floor at more than one level. That type had had no production consumer since it was written. Because of it, floor _n_'s arriving flight already continues floor _n−1_'s departing flight through the shared half-landing with no coordinate change whatever, so a walkable stair between storeys needed no new geometry and no new agreement between two storeys about where the stair is. The owner answered the open shape questions on 2026-09-13.
- decision: The building is the typical floor stacked 1…10 times at the 3.00 m floor-to-floor pitch, and a HUD stepper reading `[−] 01 [+]` is how many. `domain/storeys.ts` owns the whole arithmetic of the repetition — the bounds, the level of each storey, the inverse "which storey is this level on?", the top of the built fabric and the labels — so the stepper the owner reads, the levels the scene stacks at and the text the HUD prints cannot disagree; the pitch is read from `FLOOR_HEIGHTS` rather than written down, so a change to the section moves the whole building. The count lives in `application/floorCountStore.ts` with no bounds of its own, and an update that changes nothing returns the previous state object by identity, as the other stores do. **The pose gains `floor` and `rise` rather than one absolute height**, and this is the load-bearing choice of the part rather than a representation preference: a single float is enough to draw a camera but not to answer which storey you are on, because the storey has to be divided back out of it, and a height that lands on 3.00 m ± 1 ulp makes that quotient flicker between two answers at exactly the boundary a walker crosses — the one place the question is asked. So the discrete storey index is **stored**, `getStoreyAt` exists only for the levels nobody walked to, and `rise` is **assigned from the surface underfoot every frame and never integrated**, so it cannot drift over a climb of any length; a rise that reaches the pitch promotes the storey and carries the remainder, which is the only place the index ever moves. Collision is **two fields for the whole stack, shared by identity**: `WALK_FIELD`, the one Part 3 already swept, and `STAIR_WALK_FIELD`, the same sweep with the stair's own footprints injected as floor through the `solids` seam `getWalkField` already had — so `domain/collision.ts` did not change at all for this part. **Nothing is offset per storey**, and that is a rule rather than an economy: `isFloorBox` decides that a box is floor by comparing its top against a module constant, the finished floor level, so a solid lifted to storey 3 would be floor to nothing, the swept floor set would come out empty and the sweep would classify the whole plot as a fall. Every storey is the same floor — a point standable on storey 7 is standable on storey 3 by construction — so one pair of fields is not an approximation for the stack, it is the exact answer for all of it. What genuinely differs between storeys is only where the stair may be walked to, and only at the two ends of the stack, which is four cappings in all (lone, bottom, middle, top), so four `WalkSurface`s serve a stack of any height and `getWalkSurfaces` memoises one frozen list per count. **A flight is a height function over its footprint**, not a rectangle at a level: `domain/stairwell.ts` reads `StairPiece.surfaces` into ramps carrying the level at each end of their run and landings carrying one level, and `getStairFooting` answers the only question a walker ever asks — "what is the floor height here, and may I stand on it?" — from the point **and the rise the body is already at**, because a point in the stair bay has a set of floor heights rather than one. A body may only reach a surface within the stairwell's `reach` of its own rise, and that single rule does the work of a guard rail nobody modelled: the surfaces over a point in this bay are a full storey apart, so stepping sideways from one flight onto the one above or below it is refused by arithmetic. A storey's stairwell reaches **a full storey in each direction** — four ramps and five landings — rather than the two flights and two landings the plan draws for one storey, because from the half-landing the climb continues onto the _next_ storey's arriving flight, which this storey's own layout does not contain; without the extra pair a walker would arrive at the half-landing and find air. Rendering **draws one baked geometry once per storey** rather than holding N copies: the merged geometry now holds **storey-local** coordinates and each storey is an instance placed at its own level, so pressing the stepper mounts meshes against buffers that are already resident and re-uploads nothing. **There is no separate ceiling below the top storey**: a ceiling is the underside of the slab above, so once that storey exists a separate ceiling box is the same solid drawn twice, coincident on all six faces; the top storey, which has no slab above it, keeps one. The exterior framing takes the storey count, fits the **whole stack** and puts the orbit target at half the building's height, and `CAMERA_FAR` went 500 → 800 because the old plane was genuinely short: measured over the count × aspect matrix, the worst fog distance is 500.26 m, a quarter of a metre past it. Awareness gains the storey everywhere a room is named. `getSpaceLabel(space, floor)` yields `F1-R11/KIT · Kitchen`, matching the `F1-R11-KIT-W3` convention the wall matricules have used since ADR-010, and a room's identity becomes a `{floor, spaceId}` pair (`domain/floorSpace.ts`) rather than a `SpaceId`; the readout, the "Go to room" menu, the minimap and every failure message take their text from that one formatter, so no second spelling of a room name exists. The menu lists the rooms of **the storey the viewer is on** — twenty, never two hundred — and closes if the storey changes under an open list. **Walking to a room on another storey is refused as unreachable** rather than silently walked to the same room on this one: every storey is the same plan, so a silent substitution would be the quietest possible wrong answer, and a route finder that cannot climb must say so. The stepper is two native buttons around a polite live reading of the count, mounted in **both** views because the stack is as much an exterior fact as an interior one. Its bounds are `aria-disabled` and never `disabled`, so stepping from 4 down to 1 cannot disable the button under the user's focus on the last press and drop them onto `<body>` mid-run; the reading is a bare `aria-live` paragraph with **no** role, because an `<output>` carries an implicit `role="status"` and this HUD rations that to exactly one element, and because a `role="spinbutton"` announces its value only while the spinbutton itself has focus, where here focus lives on the buttons. Both buttons are `aria-describedby` the reading, so the current count is re-read whenever a button takes focus. Changing the count follows the owner's rule: **adding storeys never moves the viewer**, and taking them away below the viewer puts them on the new top storey keeping their plan position, pitch and yaw — `placeInStack`, applied once per change rather than once per render, returning the pose by identity when the stack still contains it — with one exception, a viewer caught mid-flight at a rise between two finished floors, who has no plan position to keep (the point they stand on is floor at their rise and thin air at the storey's own) and is returned to the arrival landing. **At one storey nothing changed**, and that is the regression gate for the whole rendering change rather than a hope: it is pinned three independent ways — the render is mesh-for-mesh identical, the framing is bit-identical, and every floor-1 camera expectation is unchanged — and both committed screenshot baselines still pass untouched. The owner's decisions of 2026-09-13 are part of this record: walking a flight **rises continuously** rather than stepping on and arriving; the maximum is 10; the stepper appears in both views and keeps your position; no separate ceiling below the top storey; both half-flights at the ends of the stack are **blocked though still drawn**, so the building shows where the stair goes without offering a storey that does not exist; the stair's walking surface is the straight line from landing to landing, accepting up to one riser of deviation from the drawn treads rather than a visible hop onto the first tread; and every room label carries its floor.
- alternatives: offsetting the solids per storey and sweeping a field for each (`isFloorBox` compares a box's top against a module constant, so lifted solids are floor to nothing, the floor set comes out empty and the sweep calls the whole plot a fall — the field would not merely cost more, it would be wrong); storing one absolute height in the pose and dividing the storey back out of it (the quotient flickers at exactly the storey boundary, which is the one place the question is asked); integrating `rise` from the vertical speed instead of assigning it from the surface (it drifts, and a stair climbed ten times is where the drift shows); a branded id string such as `F2/kitchen` as the room identity instead of a `{floor, spaceId}` pair (every consumer would parse it apart again, adding a failure path for information the caller already had in two pieces); N copies of the merged geometry in memory, one per storey (a stepper press re-uploads every buffer, for a scene whose storeys are identical by construction); a 2 cm white soffit under each slab instead of suppressing the ceiling below the top storey (it costs head height, it moves the light panels, and it changes the one-storey render — which is the baseline guarding the entire rendering change); modelling the stair surface per tread, and the nosing line through the tread corners, instead of the straight landing-to-landing line (owner decision: up to one riser of deviation from the drawn treads is preferable to a visible hop onto the first tread, and per-tread stepping contradicts the continuous rise he asked for); leaving a storey's stairwell at the two flights and two landings its own layout draws (a walker reaching the half-landing would find air where the next storey's arriving flight is); cross-floor "go to room", walking the viewer up or down to reach the pick (see the consequences: the route follower cannot drive the half-landing turn, and the honest answer is a refusal rather than a walk that goes somewhere else); silently walking to the same room on the current storey when a pick names another one (every storey is the same plan, so it would look like success and be wrong); `disabled` on the stepper's bound buttons (a disabled element cannot hold focus, so the last press of a run drops the user onto `<body>`); `role="spinbutton"` or an `<output>` for the reading (the first announces only while it has focus and promises an arrow/Home/End contract this stepper does not implement, the second brings a second `role="status"` onto a page whose screenshot mask and three `getByRole('status')` queries depend on there being exactly one); editing ADR-006 in place to delete the floor-1 clause (an ADR is history; a supersession is recorded, never backdated).
- consequences: ADR-006 stays accepted and its text is unchanged; this entry supersedes exactly one clause of it, "The app shows floor 1 only, placed at level 0". Everything else it decided stands: floors are still numbered 1…N, floor 0 is still undesigned, and the floor is still entered through the stairs. ADR-010's through-stair is **untouched** and is what made this cheap — the stair was already modelled as running through the storey, so floor _n_'s arriving flight already continues floor _n−1_'s departing flight with no coordinate change, and nothing about the stair's geometry moved for this part. Four costs are accepted and recorded. **Below the top storey the overhead surface is the slab's screed, not ceiling white**: suppressing the duplicate ceiling is what stops two coincident solids z-fighting, and the price is that only the top storey has a finished ceiling overhead. **Cross-floor routing is explicitly out of scope**, and the reason is worth stating rather than deferring silently: the route follower steers turn-then-go along a leg line with tolerances already sized at 0.05 m of slack in the tightest doorway on the floor, and a flight of this stair ends in a 180° turn on a 1.00 m landing. Driving that turn is not the same problem as walking a corridor, and a walker that scrapes a jamb on the floor would wedge in the stairwell; "Go to room" therefore refuses another storey as unreachable, and `RoomMenu.tsx` carries the note saying where the feature goes when it exists. **The dog-leg requires a strafe at the half-landing**: the two flights sit on different z strips of the bay, so a viewer walking up by hand arrives at the half-landing facing a wall and must step sideways onto the next flight — it is the building's geometry, the same move a person makes on the real stair, and not a defect to constant-tune. And **ADR-013 is corrected on one point**. It recorded that at the stairs arrival the third-person camera is pinned to its 0.500 m minimum at 45.6° elevation and that "no constant can improve it, because behind the camera is the shaft". The first half was measured and remains the arithmetic of that pose; the explanation is now false. The follow camera reads the bay-released field for the storey the viewer is on, so at the arrival landing it backs **into the open shaft** and frames the whole person there. The cost ADR-013 described was a consequence of the collision model — a camera field in which the shaft was solid — and not of the building, and the entry above records what was believed at the time, as ADR-012 and ADR-011 did before it; `docs/RUNBOOK.md` now describes what the camera does instead of quoting the 0.500 m figure as a permanent limit. Two smaller things follow from the choices. The stepper's range is nine presses end to end and deliberately has no press-and-hold repeat, no arrow keys and no shortcut key, because no role here promises that contract and every free key of the interior view is already bound. And the one-storey scene being pinned mesh-for-mesh means any future change to the merged geometry has to justify itself against a render that a stacked building must still reproduce exactly at a count of one.

## ADR-013 — Exploring the whole floor: one walk field, a walk that routes itself, a camera flight, and a keyboard-operable exterior (2026-09-12)

- status: accepted, and it supersedes ADR-002
- context: Part 2 rendered the whole floor while letting the viewer walk in one room of it. Walking was clamped to the master bedroom by `interimWalkArea.ts`, which ADR-008 introduced as an interim and promised would be deleted rather than extended, and the whole floor was therefore visible with invisible walls across every doorway. The exterior view had no keyboard controls at all and, precisely because of that, deliberately carried no role and no tab stop: ADR-002 deferred accessible camera navigation to the navigation work and said in as many words that the work must ship keyboard controls and single-pointer controls for every camera movement before it is done. ADR-008 closed the single-pointer half for the interior with the remote control and left the exterior orbit on drag and wheel, recording that the Part 3 decision would be the one to supersede ADR-002. That is this entry. Three further things forced decisions rather than following from the clamp's removal. Knowing where you are stops being free once you can leave a room, so the floor needs a room readout, a way to ask to be taken somewhere, and a map. Cutting between an orbit position tens of metres out and an eye inside a bedroom tells the viewer nothing about how the two are related, so the switch between views needs a camera travel, and a travel needs an answer for viewers who ask for less motion. And the owner asked for one thing no task line had spelled out: choosing a room must **walk** the viewer there rather than teleport them, which turns a menu into a route-finding and steering problem.
- decision: Collision is one walk field, swept once at module load, with three predicates and no special cases. `domain/collision.ts` classifies every cell of the plot by sweeping the sorted unique faces of the plot, the slabs and the wall pieces — the technique `walls.ts` already uses, exact because every face is on the centimetre grid — against a body span of 0 to `PERSON_SPEC.height`: a cell is **floor** where a box's top is the finished floor level, which takes in every slab and every door threshold block; **solid** where a box's vertical extent overlaps the body span, which takes in full walls, parapets and window sill blocks but never a threshold (its overlap is exactly zero) nor a lintel (2.10 to 2.70 m, above the head); and **fall** where it is neither, which is the two side-B voids and the 6.00 m² stair shaft. The headline rule of the part — movement crosses a wall at a port and nowhere else, never through a window — therefore **falls out of the geometry and is written nowhere**: `collision.ts` imports nothing from `domain/ports/`, and a door is passable because its threshold is floor at body height while a window is not because its sill is solid there. This is also why the walkable area is not the slab union, which is what `TASKS.md` originally asked for: the slabs stop at the wall faces, so inside a doorway there is no slab at all, and taking the union would have needed a hand-built bridge rect per door, filtered to "door, not window" — exactly the special rule the exit condition forbids. A fall edge stops the body like a wall does, a body radius short of the brink (0.25 m, and 0.275 m at the two railed balcony edges), so the viewer cannot lean out over a shaft to look down; consistency with every other obstacle won over the view. Movement resolves x then z, each axis swept rather than sampled, so an arbitrarily large step cannot tunnel a 0.15 m wall, and `stepEyePose` remains the single mover with `MovementIntent` as plain data. The automatic walk is not a second mover but an intent source competing with the keyboard and the pad for that one call: it returns signs, collision decides what happens, and it learns the outcome from the next frame's pose, which is exactly why it cannot cross a wall and why being stuck is its only failure mode. Its route extends `reachability.ts` rather than adding a second graph — the existing breadth-first walk is factored into one private traversal returning a predecessor map, `getReachableSpaceIds` becomes its key set with its contract, messages and whole test file unchanged, and `findSpaceRoute` walks the predecessors back — because a second derivation of the same edge rule is the one-detector mistake ADR-012 exists to warn about. Waypoints come from `getSlabs` rather than from `space.rects`, so the stair flights are excluded without being named, with two waypoints per crossing (one each side, because a 0.30 m wall is deeper than a 0.50 m body and a single point in the opening would have the walker turning while straddling the jambs), the crossing derived from the **contact** rather than from `getPortOpening` (which throws for the zero-gap `stairs ↔ corridor` join, and where a pair has two such contacts the widest span wins, z 4.75 rather than 5.75), and a destination taken as the centre of the largest **standing** rect — area shrunk by the body on both axes — rather than of the bounding box or of the largest rect by plain area. Both alternatives are demonstrably wrong on this floor: the bounding-box centre of the stairs, (3.60, 5.00), is inside a flight and therefore a hole, and the guest room's north strip wins on plain area, 6.08 against 4.34 m², while being only 0.75 m deep, so plain area would land "Go to guest room" in the circulation strip instead of the room; standing area puts it at (5.50, 7.825). Steering is turn-then-go with hysteresis and cross-track correction against the leg **line** rather than the leg's end point, because arcing into a 0.90 m door with 0.20 m of slack is how a walker scrapes a jamb, and every tolerance is derived from `EYE_NAVIGATION_CONFIG` rather than written down. Stuck detection is time-based rather than frame-based, so a 144 Hz machine is not given a quarter of the patience of a 30 Hz one, and after 1.5 s without progress the walk is abandoned and announced rather than re-planned: nothing on this floor moves, so a block is a geometry defect worth surfacing, and re-planning from a wedged pose invites a loop. Waypoint arrival is decided by an **along-track foot test** and not by distance alone, which is the one defect measurement found and forced a change of rule rather than of constant. Checking "am I within `arrivalRadius`" once per frame can step straight over the arrival disc: a walker drifting at the full lateral deadband crosses that disc on a chord of 0.1375 m while one worst-case step is `walkSpeed × maxStepSeconds` = 0.1400 m, which is wider. Measured, **4 of 300 walks were blocked at dt = 0.1 s against 0 of 1800 at frame times from 1/30 to 1/240**, and one traced failure came within 0.4 mm of the radius, missed it, and — because the bearing is frozen at the leg's origin, so the walker never re-aims once past a waypoint — sailed 1.87 m onward and abandoned the walk. This is not an exotic case: `dt` is capped at 0.1 s, so any frame slower than 10 fps pins it at the cap, and the interior view runs at roughly 2 fps under software WebGL, which is the very environment the required end-to-end walk runs in. Raising the radius cannot fix it, and that is the part worth remembering: at the cap a single diagonal step moves 0.099 m sideways, already wider than the arrival radius itself, so **no radius sized for a nominal frame can guarantee sampling at the worst-case frame** and only a geometric test can. A waypoint is now reached when the distance is within `arrivalRadius` **or** the projection of the pose minus the leg origin onto the leg's bearing has passed the leg's length, which is monotone in progress and therefore cannot be stepped over at any frame rate; after the fix, **0 of 2700 walks were blocked** across the same frame times including the cap. The exterior view becomes keyboard-operable and gains an on-screen pad, which is what closes ADR-002. `domain/orbitNavigation.ts` owns a spherical `OrbitPose` stepped per frame with the arrow keys orbiting and tilting and `Equal`/`Minus` plus their numpad twins zooming, chosen so nothing overlaps `EYE_KEY_BINDINGS` or `KeyV`, asserted by a test; drei's `OrbitControls` keeps pointer drag and wheel, because driving it by keyboard is a dead end — its own key handling _pans_, which would destroy the derived framing pivot, and rotate and tilt have no imperative API. The limits come from `useExteriorFraming`, so the domain clamp and the controls' clamp are the same numbers and cannot fight. The six orbit actions each get a hold-to-act button, and rather than hand-copying the roughly 120 lines of anti-stuck logic in `RemoteControl.tsx` — a pointer ledger and four release nets, where a stuck turn spins the camera forever — that logic was extracted into a generic `createHoldToActStore` and a shared `ui/HoldPad.tsx`, with both pads as thin wrappers; the extraction landed as a pure refactor, with `RemoteControl.test.tsx` and the three existing `remoteControl.spec.ts` tests unchanged and green, before `OrbitPad` was added. The exterior camera now **remembers its orbit angle** across exterior, interior and back, in `application/exteriorOrbitStore.ts`, as a target-relative pose rather than a raw position, so it survives a canvas resize where a position would not, written at three moments only — the end of a pointer drag, the frame a held key or pad button is released, and the component's unmount, which is the write that makes the memory work at all. The `hasOrbited` latch of ADR-008 is **deleted outright**: its job was to avoid overwriting the viewer's view, and a remembered pose cannot overwrite itself. The view switch flies the camera over `VIEW_TRANSITION_SECONDS` = 0.9 s of eased travel, owned by one `useFrame` in `ui/ViewTransition.tsx` mounted for the whole life of the scene **outside** the view ternary, with the ternary's two control components not mounted while a flight runs, because both write the camera every frame and would simply overwrite the interpolation. The start pose is read off the live camera, so there is no jump whatever the previous control left behind, and the hand-off rule is one sentence: the flight's endpoint expression and the mounting control's placement expression must be the same expression, which is why the exterior endpoint and `ExteriorCameraControls`' placement both read the remembered orbit pose and the interior endpoint and the explorer's own pose both come from the single `INTERIOR_START_POSE` object in `ui/floorInstance.ts` rather than from two equal computations. `prefers-reduced-motion` governs **that flight and nothing else**: under the setting the phase never leaves `'none'` and the switch is bit-for-bit the instant one the app had before flights existed, while an automatic walk is untouched in speed, route and outcome, because walking is the content and not decoration; one test pins exactly that distinction rather than a global motion switch. Interior entry always starts at the stairs arrival, (5.10, 5.00) facing the corridor, with no remembered interior pose, so the interior baseline keeps depending on one fixed pose rather than on history. Awareness is carried by the matricule. `Space` gains a required `matricule` field built in `floorPlanData.ts`, the one module already licensed to read `sourceOfTruth/plan.ts`, and one formatter `getSpaceLabel` yields `R11/KIT · Kitchen` for every consumer, so no second spelling of a room name can exist anywhere in the app, failure messages included; the owner chose matricule plus name over the bare name. The pose reaches the HUD without re-rendering React: the pose itself is a non-reactive module-level value written every frame, only the resolved room id is reactive, and the room is re-resolved after 0.05 m of movement — a distance threshold rather than a time one, so it is deterministic in tests with no clock to fake and does nothing at all while the viewer stands still or turns in place. `domain/currentSpace.ts` keeps the **last known** room whenever `findSpaceAt` returns a void or nothing, which it does constantly for the 0.15 to 0.30 m wall bands a 0.25 m body straddles, so standing in a doorway you are in the room you came from until you are in the next one and the readout never blanks or chatters. The readout is a `<p>` with `aria-live="polite"` and `aria-atomic` and deliberately **no role**, because a second `role="status"` on the page would break the HUD mask of the screenshot specs and every bare `getByRole('status')` query, and it stays mounted, empty and `sr-only` when there is nothing to say, because a live region must be in the DOM before its text changes. A successful walk announces exactly twice, where it is going and where it ended up, with the per-room line suppressed in between so that crossing three rooms does not announce three times. "Go to room" is a disclosure plus native buttons rather than a `<select>` (arrow-keying a closed select fires `change` per key on some platforms, so every arrow press would start a walk) and rather than `role="menu"` or `role="listbox"` (which promise an arrows, Home/End and typeahead contract that native buttons give for free), with the list computed once at module level because the floored plan is one connected component and a list whose items appeared and vanished as the viewer walked would be a focus hazard. Because activating a menu item starts motion the viewer did not physically drive, there are three ways to stop it: an on-screen "Stop walking" button, `Escape` while the view has focus, and any manual movement input at all, look-only included. `Escape` is not a single-character shortcut, so WCAG 2.1.4 is not engaged by it. The minimap is interior-only, since outside the whole floor is already on screen, and its rooms are **pointer-only** targets with the full-size "Go to room" menu as the keyboard and touch route to the same store action, which is the equivalent-control exception WCAG 2.5.8 allows and is the honest answer when a room is about 15 px across at any HUD-sensible size. It is hidden below the `sm` breakpoint because everything it conveys is available as text through the readout and the menu, which is the test of a legitimate responsive `hidden`. Finally, the view region is a focusable `role="application"` in **both** views, named "Interior 3D view" and "Exterior 3D view", each distinct from the toggle that switches to it, and it takes focus on entering the interior and on returning to the exterior but **never on first page load**, so no focus ring is drawn over the building on arrival (owner decision).
- alternatives: keeping the interim one-room clamp and widening it room by room (it would grow into a second, competing collision model, which is what ADR-008 said it must not become); taking the walkable area as the slab union with a per-door bridge rect (it needs a "door, not window" rule the exit condition forbids, and it puts a second hand-written description of the floor beside the swept one); sampling movement per axis instead of sweeping it (a large step then tunnels a thin wall, and the frame-time cap guarantees large steps); letting the body lean out over a fall edge since nothing physical stops an eye there (it makes the void the one obstacle on the floor that behaves differently from every other); teleporting on a room pick, which is what the task line implied (the owner asked for a walk, and a jump teaches nothing about how the rooms connect); a second route graph in its own module (two derivations of one edge rule with one detector each, the exact mistake ADR-012 records); waypoints from `space.rects` rather than from the slabs (the stair flights would then have to be named and excluded by hand); one waypoint per crossing (the walker turns while straddling a 0.30 m jamb); destinations at the bounding-box centre (the stairs' centre is a hole) or at the largest rect by plain area (it lands the guest room's destination in a 0.75 m circulation strip); raising `ARRIVAL_STEP_FACTOR` or the arrival radius to stop the sampled overshoot (measurement shows one diagonal step at the frame cap moves further sideways than the radius itself, so no radius can be large enough without being wider than a cubicle door); declaring the overshoot a test-environment problem and lowering it with a faster `walkSpeed` under test (it would hide the defect in exactly the configuration real viewers on slow machines run); frame-based stuck detection (it scales the walker's patience with the refresh rate); re-planning a blocked walk (a wedged pose invites a loop, and a block is a defect worth surfacing); driving drei's `OrbitControls` from the keyboard (its key handling pans the target, destroying the derived framing pivot, and rotate and tilt have no imperative API); a second hand-written copy of the hold-to-act logic for the orbit pad (a stuck turn spins the camera forever, and ADR-012 exists to warn about exactly this duplication); remembering the exterior camera as a raw position (a resize re-derives the framing target, so a position no longer means the same view while an angle does); keeping the `hasOrbited` latch alongside the memory (its flaw was dying with its component, and a remembered pose cannot overwrite itself); a `role="status"` for the room readout (it would break the HUD mask and every bare status query in the specs); a `<select>` or a `role="menu"` for the room list (a change of context per arrow key in the first case, a whole keyboard contract to reimplement in the second); listing only the rooms reachable from where the viewer currently stands (the floored plan is one connected component, so the list would be identical anyway while appearing to shift under the viewer's focus); offering the voids in the list and refusing them (a permanently unusable control is noise in the Tab order and in a screen reader's list of buttons); making the minimap rooms focusable (twenty-two sub-24 px tab stops through a picture, where the equivalent full-size control already exists); one global motion switch covering the flight and the walk alike (it would slow or skip locomotion the viewer asked for, and reduced motion is about decoration); focusing the view region on first load now that the exterior is focusable (a focus ring over the building before the viewer has asked for anything); renaming `INTERIOR_REGION_ID` now that it serves both views (six files across three owners for no change in behaviour); editing ADR-002 and ADR-008 in place to match what was built (an ADR is history, and a supersession is recorded rather than backdated).
- consequences: ADR-002 is superseded and its gap is closed: both views are keyboard operable, both have single-pointer on-screen controls for every camera movement, and the canvas region now carries `role="application"` in both — which ADR-002 explicitly refused while the keyboard support did not exist, and which is only honest now that it does. ADR-008 keeps its text, and two of its promises are deliberately **broken** rather than kept, both in the direction it authorised when it said that whatever Part 3 does not consume gets deleted. `createInitialEyePose`, kept there as ADR-004's corner-pose history, is **deleted** along with the single-room clamp it was built on: ADR-004's own text remains the history, which is what an ADR is for, and a dead function is a worse record than the entry describing it. `getBlockedRects` in `stairs.ts`, which ADR-008 named as speculative and expected Part 3's collision to consume, is **deleted rather than consumed**: the stair shaft has no slab, so the sweep already blocks it, and keeping the export would have been a second hand-written description of the same hole. `getReachableSpaceIds` is the opposite case — the route finder and the reachability invariant test both consume it, so it stops being dead code. `interimWalkArea.ts` and `INTERIM_WALK_SPACE_ID` are gone entirely, as ADR-008 said they would be, and `createRoomCentrePose`, `CameraRoomBox` and `createCameraRoomBox` go with them; `getThirdPersonCamera` keeps its algebra unchanged and takes a camera field instead of a room box, which means the follow camera can now follow through an open doorway instead of being trapped in one convex room. Five costs are accepted and recorded rather than dressed up. The two 0.60 m bath and shower cubicle doors keep only 0.10 m of lateral play, so walking them by hand needs the body within about 5 cm of the centre line; the automatic walk steers through the centre and is unaffected, and the source of truth is deliberately not edited to widen them, because that is not what this part is for. An oblique leg out of an L-shaped room's connector is not body-square-safe in general: the concrete case is the guest room to guest sanitair leg from (5.50, 7.05) to (7.75, 6.75), which clips the notch corner around x 6.9 to 7.1 and z 7.05 to 7.12, and it arises on **no** route from the stairs arrival, which is why the data-wide proof passes; collision still clamps, so the worst outcome is a scrape and possibly a stall-detected abandonment, never a wall crossing, and the fix — approach points either side of a connector — would change waypoint lists that tests now pin, so it is recorded rather than done. Arrival may legitimately land up to one worst-case step, 0.14 m, past a waypoint at the frame-time cap, so the assertion that the final distance is within `arrivalRadius` holds at real frame rates and relaxes by one step at the cap; a destination is a room's standing centre, not a dock. And a **live resize no longer re-fits the building**: placement used to re-derive the camera from the framing on every canvas change, so a resize always fitted the floor, whereas it now keeps the viewer's remembered angle and distance and merely re-clamps into the new limits, which is what remembering the angle means but also means that rotating a desktop window down to phone width can crop the building, since `fitDistance` depends on aspect and the remembered distance does not follow it. A fresh load at any size still fits, because the pose is seeded from that aspect's own framing, so the exposure is limited to a live resize or a device rotation; if it proves to read as broken, the contained fix is a `userAdjusted` flag in the orbit store, set when a pointer or key movement ends and never when seeding, which is not the deleted `hasOrbited` latch, whose flaw was dying with the component. The fifth cost comes with the start pose itself, and it is a framing ADR-008 explicitly rejected: it listed "keeping the corner start pose (third person then opens on a head close-up, in the very view the owner asked for)" among its rejected alternatives, and recorded that the mid-room pose it chose instead "keeps 2.433 m of its 2.50 m follow distance". Putting the viewer where a person actually arrives reintroduces the close view that alternative was rejected for, and the measurement is exact rather than approximate: the arrival landing is 1.00 m deep, so the body centre stands 0.50 m from the stair-shaft fall edge, the 0.15 m camera margin leaves 0.35 m of plan room behind it, and with yaw −π/2 putting the camera's ray at −x, `getThirdPersonCamera(INTERIOR_START_POSE, CAMERA_FIELD)` yields **0.500 m at 45.6° elevation** — `0.35 / cos 45.6°` — which is exactly `minBodyVisibleDistance`, the floor below which the mannequin is hidden. No constant can improve it, because behind the camera is the shaft: it is the building's geometry and not a defect, and the mid-room figure ADR-008 quoted is unreachable now that the pose it described no longer exists. The owner accepted it on 2026-09-12 with the mitigation in front of him: the interior **opens in first person**, so the close view appears only when `V` is pressed while still on the landing, and a couple of steps into the corridor restore the full 2.50 m follow distance. ADR-008 keeps its text here as everywhere else — this entry is the record — and `docs/RUNBOOK.md` now states the measured 0.500 m at 45.6° instead of the 2.433 m it had inherited. One naming debt is taken on knowingly: the view region keeps the id `INTERIOR_REGION_ID` although it now serves both views, because renaming it would touch six files for no behaviour change, and this sentence is the record so the next reader does not mistake it for a bug. The matricule becoming a required field on `Space` is contained, since nothing outside `domain/floorPlan/` constructs a `Space` literal and only three test helpers do; the matricule pattern shipped as `[A-Z]{3,4}` rather than the `[A-Z]{3}` first written down, because `VOID` is the one four-letter type code and the stricter pattern would have thrown on the real plan. Waypoints snap to a half-centimetre grid, because whole centimetres would shift the guest sanitair's approach point from 7.475 to 7.48 and push the body 5 mm toward the far wall of the tightest room in the building, while leaving the value raw reproduces exactly the floating-point noise the plan grid exists to prevent. The interior screenshot baseline is invalid by construction and must be regenerated, since the start pose moves from (4.10, 2.00) to (5.10, 5.00) and the interior HUD grows a readout row, while the exterior baseline **is** replaced on this branch, for a reason that is not a change in the render. Captured without the HUD, this branch and `main` produce a byte-identical exterior frame: nothing about the exterior scene changed in Part 3. The committed baseline was last written before PR #11 rebuilt the floor on the single source of truth, and that PR did not re-record it, so it had been compared against a building that no longer existed — a drift of roughly 5,000 pixels by Playwright's own metric, about 57 % of the 1 % tolerance, which is why the assertion kept passing. Part 3's camera pad then grew the masked HUD band from 80 to 176 px (122,880 px), exhausting the remaining tolerance and finally exposing that older drift: growing the mask did not change the canvas, it removed the headroom that was hiding a whole floor rebuild. The standing guard is kept without the prediction that came with it — the exterior HUD's empty, absolutely positioned live region must contribute **no** pixels, so anything added in flow to the exterior HUD band is a defect — but a moved exterior baseline is no longer evidence of that on its own, and the first question to ask of one is whether it still matches its own commit. "Settled" gains a meaning for the capture helper, which now asserts that no camera flight is pending — the region's `data-camera-transition` — before comparing two identical captures, because a 0.9 s eased flight would almost never survive the old two-captures rule and almost is not a guarantee. In third person the mannequin is absent during the 0.9 s flight back out, because the camera controls are unmounted while the flight owns the camera; it is a known nit, accepted rather than threading a flag through the most contended file of the part. The `prefers-reduced-motion` scope is now a rule to keep rather than a one-off: it governs the view flight and must not be consulted for locomotion, and the test that pins the distinction is what stops a future change from quietly turning it into a global motion switch. One tooling hazard is worth knowing and is recorded in the RUNBOOK: `pnpm typecheck` under-reports on a stale incremental cache — measured at 22 errors from plain `tsc -b` against 78 across 10 files from `tsc -b --force` on the same broken tree — so it still fails the gate correctly, but anyone verifying one file's state should force the build rather than trust the incremental output.

## ADR-012 — Two wall derivations are kept, and their divergence is accepted rather than collapsed now (2026-09-12)

- status: accepted
- context: The floor's walls are derived twice from the one source of truth. `src/features/building/domain/walls.ts` derives them for the 3D model, and `scripts/source-of-truth/walls.mjs` derives them for the generated drawing, and the two are line-for-line twins over most of their length: sixteen functions carry the same names and the same algorithm in both — `near`, `mergeSpans`, `subtractSpans`, `overlapSpan`, `outlineEdges`, `directedEnds`, `orderClockwise`, `facingSpaces`, `wallMatricule`, `backToBack`, `contactsAlong`, `assignIsolationReasons`, `distanceTo`, `nearestContactIndex`, `assignJunctionReasons` and `deriveWalls` — spanning 822 of `walls.mjs`'s 1147 lines, 411 of them non-comment (measured by summing the declaration spans). That duplication was tolerable while it was believed that the pair formed a mutual check on each other, and ADR-010's consequences said so in as many words. It does not: `verify.mjs:35` imports `deriveWalls` from `walls.mjs`, so `pnpm verify:plan` checks 5, 8, 10, 11 and 12 all run on the drawing's own derivation and can never contradict it, while the Vitest suite only ever sees `walls.ts`. Each derivation therefore has exactly one detector, and nothing compares the two against each other. They have already parted. `walls.ts:1225-1230` takes the exterior envelope from the outermost room rects — the minimum and maximum x and z over every rect in the plan — whereas `walls.mjs:926-931` takes it from `PLOT` inset by one exterior thickness. Today those agree, but only by coincidence: the outermost clear rects happen to sit exactly one exterior thickness inside the plot. Two smaller drifts are confirmed in the same pair. The docstring at `walls.mjs:681` states that isolation "only ever upgrades a plain separator: an override or a weather-exposed face already has a stronger reason", while the code it documents, at `walls.mjs:748`, upgrades a weather-exposed stretch too; the `walls.ts` twin at `:900-903` documents the same behaviour correctly. And the two `near()` helpers disagree on tolerance by three orders of magnitude — `walls.ts:319` compares `<= LENGTH_TOLERANCE` (1e-9, `planGeometry.ts:37`) and `walls.mjs:179` compares `< EPS` (1e-6, `walls.mjs:162`).
- decision: Both derivations stay for now, and the divergence is recorded here rather than repaired in this commit. The risk is stated concretely so that it is not rediscovered by accident: the envelope agreement is a coincidence of the current plan, and the first time the owner insets an outermost room — the side-B strip going 0.70 m deep instead of 0.80 m, say — the rect-derived envelope of `walls.ts` moves while the plot-derived envelope of `walls.mjs` does not. The two would then classify different faces as `exterior`, produce different `faces` strings and attach different reasons to the contacts behind them, so the generated drawing and the 3D model would describe different buildings; and neither detector would fire, because `verify:plan` only ever sees `walls.mjs` and the unit suite only ever sees `walls.ts`. Until the collapse happens, a change to either derivation is made to both, and the envelope rule is the first thing to check when a room stops touching the plot edge.
- alternatives: collapsing the duplication now, in this commit (it is estimated at −450 to −500 lines, which is a change of a different kind and a different risk from the documentation fixes this commit carries, and burying it here would make both harder to review and to revert); making `verify.mjs` import `walls.ts` instead so that one derivation serves everything (`node` strips types natively but `verify:plan` must keep running with no toolchain at all, which is exactly what ADR-010 pinned, so this needs checking rather than assuming); having `verify.mjs` import both derivations and diff them, leaving the duplication in place (it would catch the envelope divergence, but it entrenches two copies as a design instead of removing one, and doubles what every future wall change has to be written into); deleting `walls.mjs` outright and generating the drawing from `walls.ts` (that is the collapse, and it is what is being deferred, not rejected); fixing only the three confirmed drifts and leaving the structure alone (it treats the symptoms and leaves the mechanism that produced them, which is that nothing compares the two); saying nothing and leaving the false "two independent implementations" claim standing (it is the claim that made the duplication look like a safety feature, and it is what this entry exists to retract).
- consequences: The governance rule that a code-review finding is either fixed or deferred with an ADR is met by this entry, and the finding is deferred deliberately rather than forgotten. ADR-010 stays accepted with its text unchanged; a parenthetical correction is appended to its consequences, where the "two independent implementations" sentence lives, in the same style ADR-011's closure already uses there, so the original wording survives as the record of what was believed. The same correction is made in `TASKS.md`, `docs/RUNBOOK.md` and `.claude/learning.md`, each of which carried some form of the claim. What a collapse will involve is written down now while it is known, so the next session does not re-derive it: `walls.mjs` owns three surfaces that `walls.ts` has no equivalent for — `declaredOpenings`, `placeOpenings` and `wallsByRoom`, 155 lines and 82 non-comment between them — plus the `_facing` internal field and the side-letter envelope strings, so an adapter is needed rather than a straight deletion, estimated at roughly 80–120 lines. Matricule numbering is deliberately NOT on that list: `wallMatricule` already exists in both files and is one of the sixteen twins. One consequence is worth knowing before the collapse is attempted, because it decides whether the drawing can be regenerated byte-for-byte: `walls.ts` writes `faces: 'outside'` for an envelope face while `walls.mjs` writes the side-letter form `outside (side A)` from the `side: 'A' | 'D' | 'C' | 'B'` its envelope carries, so the side-letter text is the one thing a collapse would change in the generated drawing's bytes, and that change has to be made deliberately and the drawing rebuilt, not discovered as a diff. Every figure above is either a measurement stated with how it was taken or is marked as an estimate; the −450 to −500 line and 80–120 line figures are estimates and are not to be quoted as measured.

## ADR-010 — The plan is the source of truth, the drawing is generated from it, and the floor is rebuilt on plan v2 (2026-09-12)

- status: accepted
- context: ADR-003 ruled that the floor is read out of two owner-authored files, with `docs/house-design-brief.md` winning over `docs/source-of-truth-n-floor.drawio.html` and Page-2 of that drawing authoritative — the drawing read as the geometry the model must match. The owner marks his intent by hand ON the drawing, so every session the drawing was ahead of the model on some numbers and behind it on others, and no single artefact was right about the whole floor: the app carried its own hand-written copy of the plan, and two copies of a plan are two buildings, one of which the drawing was already right about and the other of which it was wrong about. ## ADR-009 — Deterministic scene lighting: the debug panel owns factors, never intensities (2026-09-12)

- status: accepted
- context: ADR-008 lights the floor with a constant three lights whose intensities differ per view (the interior raises the hemisphere fill and the ambient term, because a roofed interior with no shadow maps receives almost nothing from the sun), and keeps the Leva panel so the owner and developers can tune the scene live. The panel was wired the obvious way: each control was seeded from the intensity of the current view, `getLightingSpec(view)`. That is a trap, and it cost a session to find. Leva never re-seeds a control's _value_: the value is created from its seed the first time the control is rendered, `addData` strips `value` out of the properties it overrides even when a changed dependency array asks it to override, `useValuesForPath` lets the store win over a fresh seed, and `disposePaths` only deletes special inputs, so a plain number input survives unmounting and outlives the component for the whole life of the page. Whichever view rendered first therefore owned the light intensities from then on: entering the interior kept the exterior fill and ambient term, and the very same interior geometry settled into one of two shadings — 167929 differing pixels, a ratio of 0.19 of the frame — decided only by the order the two views had mounted in, with neither state converging to the other however long the scene was left alone. Part 2's exit asks for a rendered baseline of the interior, and a scene with two stable images cannot have one: a baseline taken then would have pinned whichever of the two the generating machine happened to produce.
- decision: The debug panel owns _factors_, never intensities. Every intensity rendered by `ui/SceneLighting.tsx` is `getLightingSpec(view)` times a factor, and every factor is seeded at `NEUTRAL_FACTOR` (1) in both views, so no control is seeded from a view-dependent value and there is nothing for Leva's store to pin; the rendered lighting is a function of `view` alone until a developer drags a control, and the spec of the current view is applied on every view change. Two folders: `Sun` (a factor on the sun plus its elevation and azimuth, both seeded from the constants of `lightingSpec.ts`) and `Sky` (the two hemisphere colours, which are view-independent, plus a factor on the hemisphere fill and one on the ambient term). A factor of 0 darkens a light completely but never removes it, so no control can change `SCENE_LIGHT_COUNT` and none can trigger the shader recompilation ADR-008 avoids. The failure mode is removed by construction rather than repaired after the fact, and `SceneLighting.test.tsx` holds it there: it asserts that every seed is view-independent against a mock that reproduces Leva's store faithfully, including `addData` dropping `value` and the store beating a fresh seed. The general rule this yields, which applies to every Leva control added from here on: a control may only be seeded from a value that does not change for the life of the page. Verified after the fix: six captures of the interior start pose taken across six separate processes and three navigation sequences — the interior entered directly, entered after the exterior had settled, and entered after a round trip out and back — produced one single hash, with all fifteen pairs at 0 differing pixels. That frame is now the committed baseline `tests/e2e/__screenshots__/exterior.spec.ts/interior-first-person-chromium-linux.png`.
- alternatives: re-seeding the controls when the view changes (this is the trap itself, not a fix: `addData` strips `value` on the override path, so the intensities never follow the view); passing a dependency array to the `useControls` schema (same mechanism, same outcome); remounting the panel per view (`disposePaths` keeps plain number inputs, so the value survives the remount anyway, and a toggle would rebuild the whole panel); dropping Leva from the lighting altogether (the panel is what ADR-008 keeps for tuning the scene, and the owner uses it); keeping absolute intensities in the panel and taking the baseline along one fixed navigation path (it pins a bug into the baseline and leaves the running app non-deterministic for the viewer, which is the actual defect); one intensity control shared by both views (the interior needs a higher fill and ambient term than the exterior, ADR-008, and a shared control would flatten one of them).
- consequences: The panel no longer shows absolute intensities: a developer reads a multiplier and looks up the value it multiplies in `lightingSpec.ts`. Because the factors are view-independent by design, a factor dragged while in one view stays applied in the other — correct for a tuning panel, and the reason the interior can no longer be tuned independently from the panel alone; an intensity that should differ per view belongs in `getLightingSpec`, not in a control. Lighting determinism is now a testable property, which is what makes the interior screenshot baseline possible at all. The rule extends to Part 4's lighting pass: shadow-map controls must be seeded from constants too. ADR-008 stays accepted; this refines its lighting decision only and changes nothing else about it.

## ADR-007 — Third-person view with a 1.80 m person (2026-09-11)

- status: accepted
- context: The owner asked to walk the chamber in third person as well as in first person. ADR-004's eye height of 1.60 m was provisional, and ADR-006 records the owner's answer that eye height follows a 1.80 m person at 1.68 m. A camera following a person indoors has to keep the body in view inside a 5.00 × 3.40 m chamber under a 2.70 m wall height, where a wall is often close behind the person (every entry starts with the back to a corner), and the switch between the two views needs the same keyboard scoping as the interior keys (WCAG 2.1.4) plus a single-pointer alternative.
- decision: Person and eye height: the person is 1.80 m tall with eyes at 1.68 m, in `PERSON_SPEC` (`domain/person.ts`). Eye height moves out of `FLOOR_HEIGHTS`, because it is a size of the person, not of the building; this replaces ADR-004's 1.60 m. Follow camera: the pure domain function `getThirdPersonCamera` places the camera about 2.5 m behind the head, 15° above it at level pitch, looking at the head, and turns it with the person. I and K tilt it within 0°–80° of elevation: looking fully up gives a level view from behind, never a view from below the head, so the camera never clips the body. The camera is pulled in along its ray to stay 0.15 m inside the chamber box (the clear rect and the floor-to-ceiling range). Rise near walls: when the pull-in leaves less than 0.5 m, the camera rises toward overhead to the lowest elevation that keeps 0.5 m, limited by the ceiling, so the body stays visible even with its back to a wall or a corner. The mannequin is hidden only if even that fails. Mannequin: a low-poly procedural figure whose parts are named ratios of the person's height, flat shaded, visible in third person only. Switch: V (physical `KeyV`, only while the "Interior 3D view" region has focus, with no modifiers and no key repeat) and a HUD toggle button "Third person" with `aria-pressed`, plus the status line "View: Interior · First person" or "View: Interior · Third person". A pointer click on the button returns focus to the view, so the keys keep working; keyboard activation keeps focus on the button. The last choice is kept across exterior ↔ interior changes, and the default is first person. The pose is stepped once per frame (frame priority −1) and shared by the camera and the mannequin.
- alternatives: a high chase camera with the walls cut away (the owner chose pulling the camera in); a top-down follow camera; a shoulder offset (at corners there are walls on both sides); a different start pose (it fixes only the entry, not every wall the person backs up to); hiding the mannequin near walls (third person then looked like first person); letting the camera go below the head (it clips the torso and flips from above to below the head near walls); easing the camera between positions (not needed once the elevation stays at or above 0°); pointer-lock mouse look (rejected in ADR-004).
- consequences: The chamber-box pull-in is replaced by wall collision in Part 3. Close to walls the view is near-overhead and shows mostly the head and shoulders. The first frame showing the mannequin compiles its shaders, which takes seconds in software WebGL; the end-to-end tests allow for it. Some screen readers (iOS VoiceOver, TalkBack) send pointer clicks, so focus also returns to the view there; this is acceptable because the view precedes the button in the Tab order and is labelled. ADR-004 stays accepted; only its eye height is superseded.

## ADR-005 — Floor plan coordinates and model (2026-09-11)

- status: accepted
- context: Part 1 turns the whole typical floor (ADR-006) into domain data that the walls, ports, collision and HUD of Parts 2–3 read. The brief gives clear sizes and depth and width chains but no coordinate system, and Page-2 gives positions in pixels. The model needs one frame, one representation for every space (including the L-shaped guest room and the side-B void, which the balcony slab cuts in two), and one rule for the wall thickness between neighbours, and it has to reproduce every brief §8 number.
- decision: Coordinates: the origin is the outer corner where sides A and C meet; x runs along A→D from 0 to 22.50, z along C→B from 0 to 10.00, y points up, and units are metres. The rendered floor sits at y 0. Each space is a set of clear (inner) axis-aligned rects on a centimetre grid, enforced by validation; comparisons use a 1e-9 m tolerance and derived lengths are rounded to the centimetre. A space has one of four kinds: room, circulation, openAir or void. The side-B void is split into `voidWest` (x 1.60–12.70) and `voidEast` (x 16.20–20.20), so every space is one connected region; brief §5.2 already names the two parts. The guest room is the net L: the gross L minus the guest-sanitair block (x 8.00–9.80, z 6.90–8.40, the sanitair's clear rect plus its 0.20 m walls, 2.70 m²). The net shape has two reflex corners with no axis-aligned chord between them, so it is modelled as three rects: x 4.00–8.00 z 6.70–8.40, x 7.20–8.00 z 5.60–6.70, and x 8.00–9.80 z 5.60–6.90. Point lookup is half-open `[min, max)`: a min face belongs to the space and a max face does not, so at a zero-gap join the point goes to the space on the + side. Join thickness follows one rule: `voidFacing` (0.30 m) when either side is openAir or void, otherwise `partition` (0.20 m); the envelope uses `exterior` (0.30 m). Explicit overrides carry their reason: stairsElevator ↔ corridor is 0 (one continuous hall, brief §4.2); voidWest ↔ balconySlabB and voidEast ↔ balconySlabB are 0 (the same open-air strip); voidEast ↔ utilityRoom is 0.20 m (ADR-006). The rule lives in domain data, not only in tests, because Part 2's wall generation reuses it. Areas are not stored in the data; the brief §8 table lives only in the tests as an independent check of the rects. `FLOOR_PLAN` is validated by tests, not at module load. The base chamber spec is derived from the master bedroom clear rect and keeps the uniform 0.20 m walls of ADR-004.
- alternatives: origin at the plan centre (every brief chain starts at an outer face, so each position would need an offset and negative values); integer centimetre storage (exact, but every reader converts to metres, and the values no longer read like the brief; a validated centimetre grid in metres gives the same exactness); a single void space (the balcony slab splits it, so it would be two disconnected regions); the gross L with the guest sanitair overlapping it (it breaks the no-overlap check and point lookup, and the net area of 10.02 m² would exist only as a subtraction); gap expectations only in tests (Part 2 would have to repeat the rule to generate walls).
- consequences: Walls, ports and collision in Parts 2–3 use these coordinates and the join rule. The side-B balcony slab (x 12.70–16.20) faces only x 14.20–16.20 of the laundry's 3.20 m side-B wall (x 14.20–17.40) and x 12.70–14.00 of the kitchen, so Part 2's laundry ↔ slab and kitchen ↔ slab doors must lie within those ranges; drawing Page-2 already places them at x 15.30–16.20 and x 12.70–13.60. A point exactly on a max face returns no space unless another space starts there with a zero gap, so callers treat "no space" as a normal result. Any new zero-gap or non-default join needs an explicit override with its reason. Because `FLOOR_PLAN` is not validated at module load, an invalid edit fails the test suite, not the running app. The §8 areas are checked but not available as data, so a later area display (Part 4 room info panel) computes them from the rects. The base chamber keeps its 0.20 m demo walls until the full floor replaces it.

## ADR-001 — Frontend stack: Vite + React + TypeScript + React Three Fiber (2026-09-11)

- status: accepted
- context: The product is a browser-only 3D building that users explore from the outside and then inside (floors, rooms, kitchen). It needs a declarative scene graph that composes with a regular UI layer (HUD, menus), fast local iteration, unit-testable logic, and an end-to-end check that WebGL actually renders. There is no backend.
- decision: Vite 8 + React 19.2 + TypeScript 6.0; three.js through `@react-three/fiber` 9 and `@react-three/drei` 10; Zustand 5 for shared UI state; Tailwind CSS 4 for the HUD; Leva for a development-only tweak panel; Vitest 5 + Testing Library (jsdom) for unit tests; Playwright (Chromium only) for end-to-end tests; ESLint 10 + Prettier. Code lives in a single feature module `src/features/building` with `domain` (pure TypeScript, no React/three) → `application` (store) → `ui` (components) seams, dependencies pointing inward; environment variables are parsed by a pure function (`src/app/parseAppConfig.ts`) that both the Vite config (fail fast at dev/build start) and the app (`src/app/config.ts`) call. Version caps: `react`/`react-dom` stay on `~19.2.8` because `@react-three/fiber` 9.7 declares the peer range `react >=19 <19.3` (19.3.0 is already published); `typescript` stays on `~6.0.3` because `typescript-eslint` 8.x declares the peer range `typescript <6.1.0` (7.x is already published). `jsdom` stays on `^29.1.1` because jsdom 30 requires Node `^22.22.2 || ^24.15.0 || >=26.0.0`, while 29.x accepts Node `^22.13.0 || >=24.0.0` and works with Vitest 5 (peer range `*`). `engines.node` is `^22.13.0 || ^24.0.0 || >=26.0.0`: the intersection of every `engines.node` range in the resolved dependency graph, bounded by Vitest 5 (`^22.12.0 || ^24.0.0 || >=26.0.0`) and ESLint 10 / jsdom 29 (`^20.19.0 || ^22.13.0 || >=24`).
- alternatives: plain three.js (imperative scene management, no component model shared with the HUD); Babylon.js (heavier runtime, weaker React integration); Next.js (server rendering adds nothing to a client-side WebGL app); Redux Toolkit (more ceremony than Zustand for small UI state); Jest (separate transform pipeline, whereas Vitest reuses the Vite config); Cypress (Playwright is faster headless and simpler to run in CI); latest React 19.3 / TypeScript 7 (outside the peer ranges of `@react-three/fiber` and `typescript-eslint`); jsdom 30 (narrows the supported Node range for no benefit to these tests).
- consequences: React and TypeScript upgrades are blocked until `@react-three/fiber` and `typescript-eslint` widen their peer ranges; the `~` ranges keep `pnpm update` from crossing the caps. Node 22 older than 22.13.0 is unsupported; moving to jsdom 30 later raises the floor to `^22.22.2 || ^24.15.0 || >=26.0.0`. The WebGL `<Canvas>` cannot run under jsdom, so the 3D scene is covered only by the Playwright smoke test. Leva is part of the production bundle (its panel is hidden outside development). three.js makes the main chunk large; code splitting is deferred until real building content exists.
